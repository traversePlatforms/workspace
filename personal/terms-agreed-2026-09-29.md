## ⚑ What escrow means, since it was the open question

**Escrow is a neutral third party holding something until agreed conditions are met.** For a
vehicle it normally means the **title document** sits with an escrow agent — a title company,
an attorney, sometimes a bank — and is released to the buyer only when the final payment lands,
or back to the seller if the deal collapses.

**So "I keep the title" and "the title is in escrow" are not the same thing, and the contract
has to say which.** There are two separate questions and they are easy to conflate:

- **Legal ownership** — whose name is on the title at the DMV.
- **Possession of the document** — who physically holds the piece of paper.

In Colorado **the title is the proof of ownership**. If it sits in escrow, Matthew may remain
the legal owner while being unable to sell it, transfer it, or borrow against it without the
escrow agent releasing it.

### ⚑ The question that decides whether escrow is safe here

**"Title in escrow" can mean two very different things, and the difference is everything:**

- **(a) The unsigned title, still in Matthew's name, held by an agent.** He remains the owner
  in every sense; they receive it only on final payment. **This is the fair version** and there
  is no reason to resist it — it protects him too, because they cannot get the document until
  they have paid.
- **(b) A title Matthew has already signed over, held pending payment.** A signed title is close
  to a bearer instrument: whoever holds it can file it. He is still the owner on paper until it
  is filed, but he has handed over the means to end that at any moment. **Much weaker, and it
  is not what "I keep the title" sounds like.**

**Ask Brandon which one, in those words, before agreeing.**

**Five things the clause has to name**, or it is not a clause:

1. **Who the escrow agent is** — and who pays them.
2. **Exactly what they hold** — the title alone, or the title plus the payments.
3. **What releases it to the buyer** — which payment, confirmed how.
4. **What returns it to Matthew** — and how fast, if the buyer defaults or walks.
5. **Who holds it if the agent goes out of business.**

## ⚑ Five things to settle before this is signable

Not legal advice — the list of things a lawyer would ask about first.

**1. The insurance and the title do not line up.** Matthew would be the titled owner while
Brandon's side carries the policy. If they let it lapse, Matthew owns an uninsured vehicle
someone else is driving; and an insurer can resist paying an owner who is not on the policy.
**Matthew is asking to be named additional insured and loss payee — that is the right ask, and
they are two different protections:**

- **Additional insured** — covers him on the **liability** side. If there is a crash and someone
  sues the owner, he is covered under their policy rather than exposed personally.
- **Loss payee** — covers him on the **physical damage** side. If the truck is totalled or
  stolen, the insurer pays *him*, or him jointly, rather than paying only the person who bought
  the policy. It protects his financial interest in the vehicle itself.

He is the owner and they are driving it, so he wants **both**. **Also ask for a certificate of
insurance naming him in both roles, and written notice before cancellation or non-renewal** —
without the notice clause, the first he would know of a lapse is after a claim.

**2. "Dual registration" needs defining.** Colorado's normal mechanism is **co-titling /
co-ownership** — two names on one title — not two registrations on one vehicle. Find out what
they actually mean, because "and" versus "or" between two names on a Colorado title changes
whether one owner can transfer it alone.

**3. The 90-day mechanical clause has no baseline.** *"Clearly as a result of my time with the
truck"* is exactly what a dispute would turn on, and with no record there is no way to show what
pre-existed. **Get a dated pre-handover inspection with photos and mileage**, and consider a
dollar cap so a single failure cannot exceed the deal.

**4. The default clause is being drafted by the other side, against Matthew.** It needs
**what counts as default, a cure period, written notice, and a stated remedy**. A default clause
with no cure period is the dangerous kind.

**5. Twelve months is double the exposure of six.** The prior finding was that the deal is
negative as drafted; doubling the term doubles the period that stays true, and **nothing in
this conversation changed the money except the deposit going down $1,000.**
