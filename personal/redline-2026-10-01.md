### Term 4 — escrow is in as a tick-box, not as a decision I made for you

`terms-agreed` recorded this as *"not understood"*, so nothing has been conceded. §3 now ends
with **check one**: Owner holds the title certificate, or an escrow agent does. The escrow
option names all five things a clause needs to be a clause — who the agent is, who pays, what
they hold, what releases it, what returns it, and what happens if the agent folds.

**The one thing it fixes outright:** the clause states the certificate is held **unsigned and
in Matthew's name**, and that Matthew does not endorse it into escrow. That is the difference
between the fair version of escrow and the dangerous one. A signed title in a third party's
hands can be filed by whoever holds it.

**✅ SETTLED 2026-10-01.** Matthew: *the only way I am doing anything escrow related is
whatever method allows me to still be the owner of the title.* §3 now says so outright, in
its own paragraph rather than as a footnote:

> Whichever box is checked, **Owner remains the owner of the Vehicle and the named owner on the
> title for the whole Term.** Owner shall not sign, endorse, assign or otherwise execute the
> title certificate, in favor of Holder, an escrow agent or anyone else, at any time before
> the purchase completes under Section 8(a). **Any escrow or holding arrangement that would
> require Owner to endorse the title before that point is outside this Agreement and Owner
> does not agree to it.**

The remaining tick-box is only about **who physically holds the unsigned certificate** —
Matthew, or an agent. Either is safe now, which is the point of writing it that way.

### ⚑ “Dual registration” cannot be done — and does not need to be

Matthew's note: it was Brandon's side's idea, *in case he gets pulled over and needs to be on
the registration.* Checked against the statute rather than assumed, and there are two findings.

**1. You cannot put a non-owner on a Colorado registration.**
**C.R.S. 42-3-113(2):** the department, on registering a vehicle, *“shall issue to the owner
two registration cards.”* Registration follows the title. The only way to add Brandon to the
registration is to add him to the **title**, which makes him a **legal co-owner** — flatly
contradicting §3, and the same exposure as the lienholder idea already rejected, arriving by a
different door. If the two names were then joined by **“AND”**, Matthew could not sell his own
truck without Brandon's signature.

**2. He does not need to be on it.**
**C.R.S. 42-3-113(6):** the registration card *“shall, at all times while the vehicle is being
operated upon a highway, be in the possession of the driver or carried in the vehicle”* and is
*“subject to inspection by any peace officer.”* **The statute asks where the card is, not whose
name is on it.** A driver stopped in someone else's vehicle needs a valid license, the
vehicle's registration card, and evidence of insurance — not his name printed on the
registration.

**So §5.1 now simply keeps the Vehicle in Matthew's name, and a new §5.6 does the job the
proposal was reaching for.** It requires three things in the truck at all times: the
registration card (which the statute requires anyway), Brandon's evidence of insurance
covering this Vehicle and him as a driver, and a signed **Authority to Operate** — the new
**Exhibit E**.

**Exhibit E is a one-page glovebox document**: the VIN and plate, Matthew named as registered
owner with his phone number, Brandon named as the authorized driver with his license number,
the authorized period, and a plain statement that the truck is not stolen and is in Brandon's
possession with Matthew's consent. There is a notary line on it — optional, and worth doing,
because a notarized page is harder to question at the roadside.

**This answers the actual worry better than the registration would have.** A registration card
with two names on it does not tell an officer that the driver has permission; a signed and
notarized authority, with the owner's phone number on it, does.

| New cost on Matthew | — | **§6.6, capped at [blank]** |

So the term doubled, the deposit fell $1,000, Matthew picked up a repair obligation, and the

asset he cannot sell, for zero.

**This is not an argument against the deal.** Matthew read the likelihood as **80/20 that they
buy it out**, and if they do, none of the above happens. It is an argument about **what the 20%
costs**, and right now the 20% costs him everything and Brandon nothing.

## Audit, 2026-10-01 — six defects found and fixed

Run `node personal/audit-agreement.mjs` after any future edit. It checks the things an edit
pass breaks: stale numbers, dead cross-references, clauses that contradict each other,
British spellings, and unbalanced markup. **All checks pass as of this build.**

**Four of the six were introduced by this redraft. Two pre-date it.**

| # | Defect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **§12(b)(3) was ambiguous.** §12(b) has *two* numbered lists — five Owner defaults and three remedies — so "12(b)(3)" could mean "failing to sign over the title" or "end the Term by written notice". In a clause setting a **cure period**, that is live | The cure sentence now names the default instead of numbering it |
| 2 | **The "the two sides mirror each other" note was false, and it ran against Matthew.** It said *neither Party may treat a breach as a default without first giving written notice and allowing the cure period to run* — but §12(a) deliberately gives **no** cure period for concealing the Vehicle, failing to return it, or using it in a crime. As written it would have handed Brandon a 10-day cure right on exactly the three things Matthew needs to act on immediately | Replaced with a paragraph stating the asymmetry **and why it exists** |
| 3 | **⚑ §13 did not survive outcome (b)** — *pre-dated this redraft*. §8(a) ends "except for Section 13, which survives"; §8(b) ended "except for Section 10". So the clause keeping this deal out of the employment relationship **died exactly when the deal failed and they still worked together** | §8(b) now survives §10 **and** §13, and §13 states its own survival "however it ends" so it cannot be dropped again |
| 4 | **Exhibit A had no mechanical section** — *pre-dated this redraft, but the new §6.6 made it matter*. §6.6 names Exhibit A as the baseline for pre-existing **mechanical** faults, and Exhibit A recorded odometer, tires, glass and floor mats. | 6 | **§5.6 revocation did not cover every ending.** It barred Owner from revoking the Authority to Operate except under §8(b) or §12(a) — silent on §11 total loss and on Holder ending it under §12(b) | The authority now **ends automatically when the Term ends, however it ends**, and Holder returns the signed Exhibit E with the Vehicle |
