Spotting mountain pine beetle

Three checks, outside in: the crown, the trunk, then under the bark. From the CSU Extension / Colorado State Forest Service fact sheet.

Green ≠ safeNeedles change 8–10 months after a successful attack. An infested tree can still look green.
Red = last yearNewly killed trees go straw-yellow to orange; old dead trees are gray.
Hatchet = proofLive beetles, larvae or galleries under the bark are the most certain sign.
1

From a distance: the crown

Look across the stand before walking up to any one tree.

  • Foliage turning yellowish to reddish through the whole crown, not just a branch. That usually shows 8–10 months after the attack.
  • Straw-yellow or orange = killed recently. Gray = dead for longer.
  • New attacks are usually next to or near trees killed before. Check the green pines beside the red ones.
  • Stressed trees go first: old, crowded, drought-struck, fire-scorched or damaged.
2

Walk around the trunk

Go all the way around, from the ground to head height.

  • Pitch tubes: popcorn-shaped blobs of resin where a beetle bored in. They can be brown, pink or white.
  • Boring dust (like sawdust) in the bark crevices and on the ground right at the base.
  • Woodpecker work: patches of bark stripped off, with the flakes lying on the ground or snow underneath.
3

Confirm under the bark

Take a hatchet and peel a small patch. Check more than one spot around the trunk.

  • Beetles, larvae (small white grubs), pupae or eggs. The most certain sign.
  • Galleries: each beetle pair cuts a vertical tunnel; the larvae tunnel off it sideways.
  • Blue-gray stained sapwood, from a fungus the beetles carry in. On a cut log it shows as wedges.

Don't be fooled

  • A pitch tube with a beetle stuck in it is a failed attack. If most of them look like that, the tree may have survived this year.
  • In dry weather a tree may not make pitch tubes at all. Look for the sawdust around the base instead.
  • Lots of round holes with no pitch = the beetles have already left. That wood is no longer a threat to other trees.
  • Ips beetles fly earlier in summer and get mistaken for mountain pine beetle. Mountain pine beetle's rear end is gently curved; Ips is sharply spined.

Adults fly: mid-June to SeptemberMost leave late July (lodgepole) and mid-August (ponderosa)Larvae wintering under the bark: the time to remove and treat

Once a tree is infested, nothing practical saves it. In an outbreak, the beetles coming out of one tree can kill at least two more the next year.

For the quote: check each tree for

  • Crown colour: green, yellow, orange/red, or gray.
  • Pitch tubes (colour, and whether beetles are trapped in them), sawdust at the base, woodpecker damage.
  • Under the bark in two places: live larvae or galleries? Blue stain?
  • Exit holes with no pitch (the beetles have gone, so the wood is safe to keep).
  • The green pines next to it. They're the most likely to be hit next.

Source: D.A. Leatherman, I. Aguayo and T.M. Mehall (Colorado State Forest Service), Mountain Pine Beetle, fact sheet 5.528, Colorado State University Extension, revised September 2011.
