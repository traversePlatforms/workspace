01

Your insurer's wildfire risk score, and what the new law makes them tell you

HB25-1182 · C.R.S. 10-4-124

Register Information-first, no premium promisesDoubles as A door opener on any street

If you've had a letter from your home insurer this year with a wildfire risk score on it, that letter is new — and it comes with a few rights most people don't know they have.

Since 1 July 2026, Colorado law requires any insurer that uses a wildfire risk model or score to price, renew or decline a homeowners policy to tell you in writing: your score, the full range of scores your property could have been given, where yours sits in that range, which features of your property drove it, and what each mitigation action would do to it. Policyholders get it with the renewal offer. Applicants get it within 15 days of a completed application.

Two parts people miss.

You can appeal it. If you think the score or the discount is wrong and you have evidence of work you've done, you appeal directly to the insurer. They have to acknowledge it in writing within 10 calendar days and give you a written decision within 30.

And you don't have to wait for renewal. If you've done mitigation work since you last renewed, you can ask for a revised score, and the insurer has 30 days to produce one.

Insurers also have to publish which actions earn a discount and how much each one is worth.

This isn't insurance advice — read your own notice and talk to your carrier. If it's useful, I'm happy to point anyone to the statute.

Lines cut, and why

  • "Your premium is going up whether you act or not." — Fear selling and a prediction we cannot support.
  • "We can get your score lowered." — The statute entitles a homeowner to a re-score, not to a better one.
  • "Book a free assessment before renewal season." — Urgency theatre, and Nextdoor penalises it.
  • "Insurers are using secret algorithms to drop Coloradans." — True-adjacent and inflammatory. The models are trade secrets under the act, but that framing invites a fight rather than a conversation.
  • "The IBHS Wildfire Prepared Home designation is named in the statute, so get certified." — Accurate, but it leads a homeowner toward a certification we cannot supply or verify for them.

Verified — enacted statute read in full

  • Effective 1 July 2026; applies to policies issued or renewed on or after that date.
  • Notice must contain all five: plain-language explanation; the range of possible scores; the property's relative position within that range; a written explanation identifying the primary features that influenced the score; and the impact of each mitigation action — §(6)(b)(I)–(V).
  • Delivery: applicants within 15 days; policyholders in the offer of renewal; non-renewed with the nonrenewal notice; and after new mitigation, a revised score within 30 days of request — §(7)(a)–(d).
  • Appeal: written acknowledgement within 10 calendar days, written decision within 30; denials forwarded to the commissioner on request — §(8).
  • Insurers must publish the discounts, the qualifying actions, the amount attached to each, and the appeal process — §(5).
  • Applies to homeowner's policies, residential condo units, multifamily housing, and the Colorado FAIR Plan — §(9), §(10).

Corrected during research

  • The 10-day / 30-day appeal timeline is repeated by insurance-agency blogs and an SEO insurance-news site. Rather than cite those, the figures come from the statute, which confirms them and specifies calendar days.
  • Secondary sources describe the notice as arriving "at application, renewal, or nonrenewal." The statute is more specific and more useful — the re-score on request in §(7)(d) appears in no secondary source read.

Unresolved

  • Whether the Division of Insurance has adopted implementing rules under §(11). Not checked.
  • What the notices actually look like. Six weeks of them have landed; nobody at Sixfold has seen one. A real anonymised example would sharpen every line of this post.
  • Deliberately unused: any figure for what a discount is worth. Each insurer publishes its own; there is no statewide number and inventing one is the exact failure the research standard exists to prevent.

The state pays for this work, and almost nobody claims it

C.R.S. 39-22-543 · the wildfire mitigation credit

Register Descriptive about tax, never advisoryDoubles as The strongest door opener of the five

Colorado has an income tax credit that covers 100% of what you pay a contractor for wildfire mitigation work on your own land, up to $1,000. In tax year 2023 — the most recent year with data — about 450 people in the entire state claimed it.

The state's own evaluators went looking for why. They surveyed landowners and mitigation contractors, and fewer than half had heard of the credit before the survey asked them about it.

Three things worth knowing if you're weighing up work this year.

It only covers what you pay someone else. Since August 2024, buying or renting equipment and doing the work yourself doesn't qualify — it has to be a third-party contractor's invoice, and you attach the receipts to your return.

Right now it can't exceed what you owe. If your Colorado tax bill is $600, a $1,000 credit is worth $600 to you. The remainder isn't refunded and can't be carried forward.

That changes for work done in 2027. The cap doubles to $2,000, the income limit rises to $300,000, and the excess becomes refundable — paid out even if it's more than you owe.

None of this is tax advice, and your accountant is the right person to confirm any of it. But if you're deciding between this year and next, the difference is worth knowing before you decide.

  • "We handle the paperwork for you." — We can supply an invoice and a written scope. We cannot file anyone's return.

Verified — enacted statute, DOR guidance, legislative evaluation

  • Tax years 2027–2030: credit up to $2,000, adjusted gross income at or below $300,000 — §(4)(c).
  • Refundability: before 1 Jan 2027, excess is neither refunded nor carried forward — §(5)(a). From 1 Jan 2027, excess is refunded — §(5)(b).
  • Qualifying measures, which must meet or exceed CSFS or DFPC standards: defensible space; fuel breaks; thinning woody vegetation to reduce risk to structures; and secondary treatment by lopping and scattering, piling, chipping, removing from site, or prescribed burning.
  • Non-qualifying: equipment purchase or rental for own use; inspection or certification fees; in-kind contributions; cost-sharing between landowners; and any cost paid from or reimbursed by a grant.
  • Claimed on DR 0104CR with the DR 0104, with copies of receipts.
  • Evaluation: about 450 claims in TY2023; ~$200,000 to 457 landowners; survey of 360 respondents (241 landowners, 28 mitigation contractors) — fewer than 50% aware before the survey, over 70% saying landowners are generally unaware.
  • Since August 2024, only costs paid to a third-party service provider qualify.

Corrected — and all three also correct our own files

  • The 2027 change is narrower than our files say. Both the research file and the marketing plan record HB26-1289 as having "added thinning woody vegetation." Thinning was already qualifying under §(2)(d). The bill adds a separate, narrower category — thinning of vegetation at risk of or killed by mountain pine beetle or spruce beetle. The plan's "precisely Sixfold's core service" overstates it.
  • The fiscal note contradicts the enacted act. It says the credit "may be carried forward for five years"; the statute says excess "may not be carried forward and is refunded." The note describes a version that did not survive.
  • The DOR guidance page is stale. Its table still shows 2027 at $1,000 with a "TBD" limit, and still describes a pass-through route. Use the statute for 2027 figures.

Unresolved

  • The 2026 income limit is genuinely unpublished — the DOR table says "TBD." The honest answer to a homeowner is that it has not been announced.
  • Whether "adjusted gross income" in §(4)(c) means federal AGI. The statute drops the word "federal" that the earlier subsection uses. Different number for some filers — worth an accountant's read before print.

The first five feet

Zone 1 · the 0–5 ft noncombustible strip

Register Gives the work away for freeDoubles as The driveway conversation, almost verbatim

If you only do one thing to your property this year, the research says do the first five feet.

A study of more than 71,000 properties inside wildfire perimeters between 2016 and 2019 found that buildings with a lot of vegetation within five feet of the walls were destroyed 78% of the time — close to twice the rate of buildings with little vegetation in that strip. The work is done by embers, not by a wall of flame. They land against the base of the house, find something that burns, and the house does the rest.

Colorado's forest service calls that strip Zone 1: the ground from the wall out to five feet. The guidance is unsentimental about it. Nothing in there should burn. That means pine needles cleared from the foundation and the deck, wood mulch swapped for gravel or rock, firewood moved out to 30 feet, and the first five feet of any wooden fence that touches the house replaced with something that doesn't.

That last one surprises people. A wooden fence attached to a house is a fuse with a handle on it.

None of this needs a contractor. It needs a Saturday and a wheelbarrow, and it's worth more than anything else you could spend the same day on.

  • "90% of homes lost in wildfires ignite from embers." — The most-repeated statistic in this trade, not traced to a source that survives checking. The mechanism is described without a number attached.
  • "Book a free five-foot assessment." — The post is stronger giving the work away.
  • "Your insurer will reward this." — The discount is the insurer's to define. Cross-reference post 01 instead of promising.
  • "Zone 0." — California's term. Colorado's forest service numbers the same ground Zone 1, and the wrong vocabulary makes a local post feel imported.

Verified — IBHS and CSFS, first-party

  • IBHS release, 8 Apr 2021 (Zesty.ai study): 71,000+ properties, 2016–2019, computer vision on satellite and aerial imagery. Heavy vegetation within 5 ft → destroyed 78% of the time, "nearly twice as high" as those with little.
  • Second finding, unused: other structures 30–100 ft away in moderate-to-high vegetation → 60% destroyed versus 31% for isolated homes.
  • IBHS: all combustible items must be removed "even if they are labeled 'fire-resistant'" because "embers exploit the weakest link." Wood mulch named as "highly combustible."
  • CSFS checklists: pine needles cleared from a 5-ft radius of foundation and deck; combustible fencing removed within 5 ft; firewood and chipped slash 30 ft or more; 6 inches of vertical clearance under siding.
  • CSFS zones: Zone 1 0–5 ft, Zone 2 5–30 ft, Zone 3 30–100 ft.

Corrected during research

  • IBHS overstates its own study in its headline. "Cuts risk in half" reads as a promise about one house; the finding underneath compares destruction rates between two groups. The post uses the comparison.
  • Zone numbering. Early drafting used "Zone 0" on the strength of national coverage. Colorado's zone numbers are not California's — using the wrong set in local copy is an immediate tell.

Unresolved

  • Whether the Zesty.ai study was ever peer-reviewed. What was read is the IBHS release describing it, and the study was run by a commercial vendor whose product is the wildfire score insurers buy. Not disqualifying, but disclose it if this is ever cited in print.
  • The IBHS 2018 Near-Building Noncombustible Zone report — likely the strongest source for this post, not opened. Worth retrieving before this becomes a web page.

What happens to the pile, and why the month matters

Slash disposal · beetle flight timing

Register Practical; no funding angle at allDoubles as The conversation at a slash site

If you've had pines thinned and the branches are still stacked in a pile out back, the pile is now the decision.

Fresh green pine slash is habitat. Ips beetles are active in Colorado from as early as late February through November and get through two to four generations in a season, and the state's guidance on them is blunt: don't stack green or infested conifer wood next to living conifers. Mountain pine beetles emerge from mid-June through September, with most leaving the tree in late July and mid-August. A pile of green rounds sitting through those months, ten feet from healthy trees, is doing something you didn't intend.

There are four honest things to do with cut material, and they cost different amounts: chip it, scatter it thin so it dries fast, haul it off the property, or cut it to rounds and season it well away from standing trees. Chipping and debarking both destroy the inner bark the larvae live in. Piling and forgetting is the only genuinely bad option.

It's also why winter is the better month to cut. The beetles aren't flying, the ground holds equipment better, and anyone doing this work has an open calendar between November and February that they will not have in April.

Happy to explain any of the four if you're staring at a pile.

  • "Sap is down in winter, so the trees recover better." — Arborist folklore; no source found that survives checking.
  • "Book now before spring fills up." — Urgency theatre. The open-calendar point is made as a fact about the trade, not a countdown.
  • "Your slash pile is a fire hazard." — True of a pile near a house, but stacking two hazards in one paragraph makes both sound like sales pressure.
  • "We can chip it for you." — Held. Nextdoor treats the offer to quote as advertising.

Verified — CSU Extension 5.558 and CSFS 5.528

  • Ips Beetles (Cranshaw & Leatherman, rev. Aug 2025): adults active at 50–60°F through November, observed as early as late February; two to four generations a year.
  • "Never stack green or infested coniferous wood next to living coniferous trees." Green material should be chipped or debarked — larvae do not survive either. Scattering rather than piling promotes drying.
  • Mountain Pine Beetle (5.528): emergence mid-June through September, the great majority exiting late July (lodgepole) and mid-August (ponderosa).
  • Infested logs: peel the bark, burn or scorch preferably with snow on the ground, bury under 8+ inches of soil, or chip. After emergence the wood is safe.

  • "Thinning and slash treatment should only occur from October through April." Returned confidently by search and reads as CSFS guidance. The CSFS fact sheet was read in full and contains no such window — its only April–July dates refer to preventive insecticide spraying, a different operation. The nearest source carrying it was a commercial pine-beetle services site. Not used.
  • An early draft attributed that rule to CSFS in body copy — precisely the failure mode this project has been caught by before.

Unresolved

  • Whether CSFS or CSU publishes a defensible cutting-season window anywhere. One call to a district forester converts this post's central argument from inference to citation.
  • Local slash-disposal options and fees beyond the JeffCo SLASH sites already in the research file.
  • Held back deliberately: the 2027 beetle-thinning credit, which fits this topic best but would make a practical post into a second funding post. Flagged as the January–February follow-up.

It was a grass fire, in December, and it took 1,084 homes

The Marshall Fire · 30 December 2021

Register Hardest of the five to keep out of fear sellingDoubles as The answer to "we're not in the mountains"

The most destructive fire in Colorado history didn't happen in a forest, and it didn't happen in fire season.

The Marshall Fire started on 30 December 2021, in cured grass near Marshall Road, with relative humidity around 20% and wind gusts running 70 to 100 mph. It reached Superior and Louisville in under an hour. By the next morning it had burned about 6,000 acres — small, as fires go — and destroyed 1,084 homes and seven commercial buildings, with another 149 homes damaged. Two people died. It snowed the following afternoon.

What made it possible was ordinary. A wet first half of 2021 grew tall, thick grass. Then July through December turned out to be the second warmest and by far the driest stretch in Denver's records since 1872. Fuel, then drought, then a windstorm.

The reason to bring it up isn't to alarm anybody. It's that "we're not in the mountains, so this isn't about us" is the most common thing homeowners say about wildfire on the Front Range, and Marshall is the reason it doesn't hold. Grass, a fence line, a mulch bed and a wind event are enough.

If your yard backs onto open space, the same five feet around the house matters here as it does in Evergreen.

Verified — Boulder County after-action report, read in full

  • Gusts of 70 to 100 mph at the base of the foothills, from a mountain wave.

  • ~6,000 acres by the morning of 31 December; four to eight inches of snow fell that afternoon.
  • A wet first half of 2021 produced "lush and tall grass growth"; 1 July – 29 December 2021 ranked 2nd warmest and by far the driest in Denver's records since 1872.
  • Crews "put out small fires in yards, on decks, on fences, trees, but efforts were overcome"; wind made it "difficult to stand up straight or open vehicle doors."

Quoting one as the other is how this gets misreported. The post uses the breakdown and says which is which.
  • "6,000 acres" is the size estimated on the morning of 31 December, not a final mapped perimeter. Stated as such.

What the research changed, and what is still out of reach

Findings that affect files outside this folder

Verification for these posts went to enacted statute rather than summaries. Three findings correct our own files, and one of those sits inside a document that is already published as an artifact.

Fix before either file is reused

  • The 2027 tax credit change is narrower than recorded. The research file and the marketing plan both say HB26-1289 "added thinning woody vegetation." It was already qualifying; the bill adds a narrower beetle-specific category. The plan's line calling this "precisely Sixfold's core service" overstates it.
  • The credit is neither refundable nor carry-forward until tax year 2027 — excess over your tax liability is simply lost. This appears in none of our files and it is the most commercially useful fact for a homeowner deciding between this year and next.
  • Don't use the HB26-1289 fiscal note for this bill; it contradicts the enacted act on refundability.

The marketing plan is published as an artifact, so fixing the .md means republishing to the same URL. Not done — awaiting the call.

Two claims refused

The ubiquitous "October–April cutting window" credited to CSFS is not in the CSFS fact sheet, which was read in full. "90% of homes ignite from embers" traces to no readable primary source. Neither is in any post.

Highest-value retrievals still outstanding

  • FEMA's Marshall Fire assessment team guide. 403s to a browser user agent. Would upgrade posts 03 and 05 together — it is the best existing source on why individual Front Range houses burned or survived.
  • A real HB25-1182 risk-score notice, anonymised, from any homeowner willing to share one.
  • A CSFS district forester on cutting-season timing — one phone call, and post 04's argument becomes a citation.
  • The IBHS 2018 Near-Building Noncombustible Zone report, before post 03 becomes a web page.
