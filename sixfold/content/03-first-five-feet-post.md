# Post 3 — The first five feet

**Topic:** Zone 1 / the 0–5 ft noncombustible zone — the highest-value, lowest-cost defensible space work
**Audience:** Front Range homeowners, including plains and suburban properties
**Register:** Friendly neighbourhood, information-first. The one post that gives away useful work for free.
**Platform fit:** Nextdoor, local/HOA Facebook groups. Also the driveway conversation, almost verbatim.
**Status:** Draft. Not published, not posted, not scheduled.

---

## The post

If you only do one thing to your property this year, the research says do the first five feet.

A study of more than 71,000 properties inside wildfire perimeters between 2016 and 2019 found that buildings with a lot of vegetation within five feet of the walls were destroyed 78% of the time — close to twice the rate of buildings with little vegetation in that strip. The work is done by embers, not by a wall of flame. They land against the base of the house, find something that burns, and the house does the rest.

Colorado's forest service calls that strip Zone 1: the ground from the wall out to five feet. The guidance is unsentimental about it. Nothing in there should burn. That means pine needles cleared from the foundation and the deck, wood mulch swapped for gravel or rock, firewood moved out to 30 feet, and the first five feet of any wooden fence that touches the house replaced with something that doesn't.

That last one surprises people. A wooden fence attached to a house is a fuse with a handle on it.

None of this needs a contractor. It needs a Saturday and a wheelbarrow, and it's worth more than anything else you could spend the same day on.

## Lines cut, and why

- **"Clearing five feet cuts your risk of ignition in half."** — This is how IBHS headlined it, and it is looser than the underlying finding. The study compares destruction rates between two vegetation groups; "cuts your risk in half" reads as a promise about one specific house. The post gives the actual comparison instead.
- **"90% of homes lost in wildfires ignite from embers."** — The most-repeated statistic in this trade and the one every content farm carries. Not traced to a source that would survive checking. Left out; the mechanism is described without a number attached.
- **"Book a free five-foot assessment."** — The post is stronger giving the work away. A homeowner who clears their own Zone 1 and then looks at the juniper line at 20 feet is a better conversation than one who was sold at.
- **"Your insurer will reward this."** — Plausible, and specifically the sort of thing HB25-1182 is meant to make true, but the discount is the insurer's to define. Cross-reference to post 1 instead of a promise here.
- **"Zone 0."** — Cut deliberately. It is California's term and the framing most articles use; Colorado's forest service numbers the same ground **Zone 1**. Using the California vocabulary in a Colorado group makes the post feel imported.

## Fact-check log

**Verified — IBHS news release, 8 April 2021, "Nearly Doubling a Property's Wildfire Survival Rate"** (Zesty.ai study in collaboration with IBHS):

- **More than 71,000 properties** within wildfire perimeters, **2016–2019**, assessed by computer vision on high-resolution satellite and aerial imagery.
- Buildings with a **high amount of vegetation within 5 feet** were destroyed **78% of the time** — "nearly twice as high" as those with small amounts of perimeter vegetation.
- A second finding, not used in the post: properties with other structures 30–100 feet away in moderate-to-high vegetation areas were destroyed **60%** of the time versus **31%** for isolated homes.
- Quoted: Attila Toth (Zesty.ai CEO), Roy E. Wright (IBHS President and CEO).

**Verified — IBHS Wildfire Prepared Home, "Will It Burn?"** (wildfireprepared.org):

- The 0–5 ft zone should be noncombustible, and **all combustible items must be removed "even if they are labeled 'fire-resistant'"** — the stated reason being that "embers exploit the weakest link."
- Named as combustible and to be removed: plants, **wood mulch** ("highly combustible"), trash and recycling bins. Named as acceptable: solid concrete ornaments, solid metal furniture.

**Verified — Colorado State Forest Service Home Ignition Zone checklists** (csfs.colostate.edu):

- Clear pine needles from a **5-foot radius around the foundation and deck**.
- Remove combustible fencing **at least within 5 feet of the home**.
- Firewood **at least 30 feet** from the home; chipped slash accumulations **30 feet or more**; propane tanks **at least 30 feet**.
- **6 inches of vertical clearance** between the ground and the siding.
- Shrub spacing **at least two-and-a-half times their mature height**.

**Verified — CSFS zone distances:** Zone 1 is **0–5 feet**, Zone 2 is **5–30 feet**, Zone 3 is **30–100 feet**. This is the Colorado numbering and it differs from the California "Zone 0/1/2" scheme that most national coverage uses. Zone 1 "requires the most vigilant work in order to reduce or eliminate ember ignition and direct flame contact."

**Corrected during research:**

- **The IBHS headline overstates its own study.** The release is titled "…cuts its risk of igniting from a wildfire in half," and the CEO quote says "cut risk nearly in half," but the finding underneath is a comparison of destruction rates between two groups of properties (78% versus roughly half that). Written the loose way it becomes a promise about an individual house. **The post uses the comparison, not the headline.**
- **Zone numbering.** Early drafting used "Zone 0" throughout, on the strength of national articles and the IBHS materials. CSFS numbers the same 0–5 ft strip **Zone 1**. Corrected throughout, and worth a standing note: **Colorado's zone numbers are not California's**, and using the wrong set in local copy is an immediate tell.

**Deliberately not used:**

- **"Embers cause 90% of home losses"** and its variants. Ubiquitous, attributed loosely to IBHS, NFPA and USFS depending on who is repeating it, and not traced to a readable primary source. Exactly the pattern the research standard was written for.
- **The IBHS 2018 Near-Building Noncombustible Zone report.** Cited in search results and likely the strongest source available for this post, but it is a PDF that was not opened. **Worth retrieving before this becomes a web page** — it would let us describe the test conditions rather than the summary.
- **Any figure for what Zone 1 work costs.** Because the honest answer in the post is that it doesn't need a contractor.

**Unresolved:**

- **Whether the Zesty.ai study was ever published in peer-reviewed form.** What has been read is the IBHS release describing it. The methodology (computer vision on aerial imagery, 71,000 properties) is stated but not independently verified, and the study was conducted by a commercial risk-modelling vendor whose product is the wildfire score insurers buy. **That is not disqualifying, but it should be disclosed if this is ever cited in print** — the same way we disclose the InsideSales authorship on the HBR lead-response figure.
- **Whether CSFS has published updated HIZ guidance since the 2021 guide.** The checklists page is current; the linked guide PDF is dated 2021.
