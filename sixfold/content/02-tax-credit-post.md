# Post 2 — The state pays for this work, and almost nobody claims it

**Topic:** Colorado wildfire mitigation measures income tax credit (C.R.S. 39-22-543) — how it works now, what changes in 2027
**Audience:** Front Range homeowners considering or deferring mitigation work
**Register:** Friendly neighbourhood, information-first. Descriptive about tax, never advisory.
**Platform fit:** Nextdoor, local/HOA Facebook groups. The strongest door-conversation opener of the five.
**Status:** Draft. Not published, not posted, not scheduled.

---

## The post

Colorado has an income tax credit that covers 100% of what you pay a contractor for wildfire mitigation work on your own land, up to $1,000. In tax year 2023 — the most recent year with data — about 450 people in the entire state claimed it.

The state's own evaluators went looking for why. They surveyed landowners and mitigation contractors, and fewer than half had heard of the credit before the survey asked them about it.

Three things worth knowing if you're weighing up work this year.

It only covers what you pay someone else. Since August 2024, buying or renting equipment and doing the work yourself doesn't qualify — it has to be a third-party contractor's invoice, and you attach the receipts to your return.

Right now it can't exceed what you owe. If your Colorado tax bill is $600, a $1,000 credit is worth $600 to you. The remainder isn't refunded and can't be carried forward.

That changes for work done in 2027. The cap doubles to $2,000, the income limit rises to $300,000, and the excess becomes refundable — paid out even if it's more than you owe.

None of this is tax advice, and your accountant is the right person to confirm any of it. But if you're deciding between this year and next, the difference is worth knowing before you decide.

## Lines cut, and why

- **"Stack it with your county's cost-share and get most of the job paid for."** — Two problems. Costs paid or reimbursed by a grant or cost-share **do not qualify** for the credit, so the stacking is far narrower than it sounds; and Sixfold is not on any approved contractor list yet, so pointing people at county money produces a dead lead. Plan §1 hard rule.
- **"You'll get $2,000 back in 2027."** — Tax advice from a tree company. The post states the statutory terms and sends the reader to their accountant.
- **"Wait until 2027 — it's worth twice as much."** — Advises deferring fire mitigation for a tax reason, which is against the reader's interest in a bad fire year. The post lays out the difference and lets them decide.
- **"We handle the paperwork for you."** — We can supply an itemised invoice and a written scope. We cannot file anyone's return, and saying it loosely reads as tax preparation.
- **"Over a million buildings sit in Colorado's wildland-urban interface."** — Kept as context, cut from the body. True, but set next to the 450 claims it invites a comparison of two different denominators. It does the argument's work too cheaply.

## Fact-check log

**Verified — enacted statute text read in full** (HB26-1289 Final Act PDF, leg.colorado.gov bill file 116779, amending C.R.S. 39-22-543):

- **Tax years 2027–2030** (years beginning on or after 1 Jan 2027 and before 1 Jan 2031): credit up to **$2,000**, landowner **adjusted gross income at or below $300,000**, inflation-adjusted thereafter — §(4)(c).
- **Refundability:** for years beginning **before** 1 Jan 2027, excess is **neither refunded nor carried forward** — §(5)(a). For years beginning **on or after** 1 Jan 2027, excess **may not be carried forward and is refunded** — §(5)(b).
- **New defined term, "infestation mitigation measures"** — §(2)(a.5). See correction 1 below.
- Statutory repeal date moved from 1 Jan 2030 to **1 Jan 2040** — §(6). Separate from the credit amounts, which §(4)(c) specifies only through tax year 2030.
- Credit amount is the same for a single filer and for two filing jointly — §(3)(a).

**Verified — Colorado Department of Revenue, *Income Tax Topics: Wildfire Mitigation Measures*** (fetched with a browser user agent; the page 403s to a plain fetch):

- Credit by year: 2023 — $120,000 FTI limit, 25%, $625 cap; 2024 — $126,300, 25%, $625; 2025 — $129,200, **100%, $1,000**; 2026 and 2027 rows show limit "TBD", 100%, $1,000.
- Qualifying measures, which must **meet or exceed Colorado State Forest Service or Division of Fire Prevention and Control standards**: creation of defensible space around structures; establishment of fuel breaks; thinning of woody vegetation for the primary purpose of reducing risk to structures; and secondary treatment of woody fuels by **lopping and scattering, piling, chipping, removing from the site**, or prescribed burning.
- Non-qualifying: the purchase price of any property; equipment purchase or rental for the landowner's own use; inspection or certification fees; in-kind contributions and donations; cost-sharing arrangements between landowners; and **any cost paid from, or reimbursed by, a grant or incentive**.
- Only a **landowner who is owner of record of private land** in Colorado. Not public land, regardless of easement or lease.
- Claimed on the **Individual Credit Schedule (DR 0104CR)** with the **DR 0104**, with **copies of receipts** attached or filed through Revenue Online.

**Verified — Colorado General Assembly 2026 tax expenditure evaluation (2026-TE4), PDF read directly:**

- "In Tax Year 2023, the most recent year of data available, there were about **450 claims** for the credit."
- The credit "reimbursed about **$200,000 to 457 landowners**."
- Survey: **360 responses**, including **241 private landowners or leaseholders** and **28 wildfire mitigation contractors**. **Fewer than 50%** were aware of the credit before the survey; **115** first learned of it from the survey; **under 11%** described themselves as very familiar; **over 70%** said landowners are generally unaware.
- Since **August 2024** (HB24-1036), only costs **paid to a third-party service provider** qualify; landowner purchases or rentals of materials and equipment do not — §39-22-543(2)(a), C.R.S.

**Corrected during research — three, and all three also correct our own files:**

1. **Our research file and the marketing plan both say the 2027 change "added thinning woody vegetation as qualifying work."** That is wrong, and it overstates the change in Sixfold's favour. Thinning of woody vegetation for the primary purpose of reducing risk to structures was **already** qualifying under §(2)(d). What HB26-1289 adds is a **separate, narrower** category — "infestation mitigation measures," meaning thinning of woody vegetation **at risk of, or killed by, mountain pine beetle or spruce beetle**, to CSFS standards. **Fix in `research/fire-mitigation-market-research.md` and `marketing/marketing-plan.md` before either is reused.**
2. **The HB26-1289 fiscal note (12 June 2026) says the bill "allows the credit to be carried forward for five years."** The enacted statute says the opposite: excess "may not be carried forward and is refunded." The fiscal note describes a version that did not survive. **Enacted text wins; the fiscal note is not a safe source for this bill.**
3. **The DOR guidance page is stale.** Its table still shows tax year 2027 at $1,000 with a "TBD" income limit, and it still describes a pass-through-entity route to the credit. HB26-1289 sets 2027 at $2,000 with a $300,000 AGI limit, and the evaluation notes eligibility narrows to **individual taxpayers from tax year 2026**. Use the statute for 2027 figures, not the DOR page.

**Deliberately not used:**

- **The $3,200–$6,100 one-acre defensible space benchmark.** Real and CSFS-sourced, but publishing a job cost before Sixfold has priced ten real jobs invites a quote we may not honour. Plan §5.
- **Any county cost-share programme by name.** Hard rule until listing.
- **RMIIA premium-increase figures** from the evaluation. Real, but mostly hail-driven — using them here would imply a wildfire causation the source does not support.

**Unresolved:**

- **The 2026 federal-taxable-income limit** is genuinely not yet published — the DOR table says "TBD." If a homeowner asks what this year's limit is, the honest answer is that it has not been announced. (The 2027 limit is superseded by the statute's $300,000 AGI figure.)
- **Whether "adjusted gross income" in §(4)(c) means federal AGI.** The statute says "adjusted gross income" where the earlier subsection says "federal taxable income." Our own files say "federal AGI." It is a different number for some filers — **worth an accountant's read before this goes in print.**
