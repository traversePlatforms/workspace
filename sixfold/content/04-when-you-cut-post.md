# Post 4 — What happens to the pile, and why the month matters

**Topic:** Slash disposal and cutting season — beetle flight timing, the four disposal choices, and why winter is the better booking
**Audience:** Front Range homeowners with pines, especially those who have already had trees cut
**Register:** Friendly neighbourhood, information-first. The most practical of the five; no funding angle at all.
**Platform fit:** Nextdoor, local/HOA Facebook groups. Strong at a slash site.
**Status:** Draft. Not published, not posted, not scheduled.

---

## The post

If you've had pines thinned and the branches are still stacked in a pile out back, the pile is now the decision.

Fresh green pine slash is habitat. Ips beetles are active in Colorado from as early as late February through November and get through two to four generations in a season, and the state's guidance on them is blunt: don't stack green or infested conifer wood next to living conifers. Mountain pine beetles emerge from mid-June through September, with most leaving the tree in late July and mid-August. A pile of green rounds sitting through those months, ten feet from healthy trees, is doing something you didn't intend.

There are four honest things to do with cut material, and they cost different amounts: chip it, scatter it thin so it dries fast, haul it off the property, or cut it to rounds and season it well away from standing trees. Chipping and debarking both destroy the inner bark the larvae live in. Piling and forgetting is the only genuinely bad option.

It's also why winter is the better month to cut. The beetles aren't flying, the ground holds equipment better, and anyone doing this work has an open calendar between November and February that they will not have in April.

Happy to explain any of the four if you're staring at a pile.

## Lines cut, and why

- **"Cut between October and April — the state says so."** — Cut, and this one is worth remembering. The October–April thinning window is widely repeated online and reads as official CSFS guidance, but it does **not** appear in the CSFS mountain pine beetle fact sheet, which is where it is usually credited. The post argues the same timing from verified beetle flight periods instead of borrowing an unverifiable rule.
- **"Sap is down in winter, so the trees recover better."** — Standard arborist folklore, and possibly true, but no source was found that would survive checking. The seasonal argument stands on beetle flight, ground conditions and contractor availability, all of which are defensible.
- **"Book now before spring fills up."** — Urgency theatre. The open-calendar point is made as a fact about the trade, not as a countdown.
- **"Your slash pile is a fire hazard."** — Cut for accuracy as much as tone. A slash pile close to a house genuinely is one, but the post is about beetles, and stacking two different hazards in one paragraph makes both sound like sales pressure.
- **"We can chip it for you."** — Held. The post ends on an offer to explain, not to quote. Nextdoor treats the second one as advertising.

## Fact-check log

**Verified — CSU Extension Fact Sheet 5.558, *Ips Beetles*** (Cranshaw & Leatherman; published December 2002, reviewed August 2025):

- Adults emerge in spring when daytime temperatures reach **50–60°F** and remain active **through November**; observed entering trees on warm days **as early as late February**.
- Colorado sees **two to four generations** annually.
- "Freshly-cut material that results from pruning or thinning practices (called 'slash') should be removed from the vicinity of valuable trees."
- **"Never stack green or infested coniferous wood next to living coniferous trees."**
- Green material should be **chipped or debarked** so the inner bark is destroyed — "Ips larvae will not survive standard chipping or debarking treatments."
- **Scattering rather than piling** promotes rapid drying and is offered as a management option.

**Verified — CSFS / CSU Fact Sheet 5.528, *Mountain Pine Beetle*** (PDF read directly):

- Emergence of new adults **begins mid-June and continues through September**; "the great majority of beetles exit trees during late July (lodgepole pine) and mid-August (ponderosa pine)."
- Larvae overwinter under the bark, metabolising glycerol as antifreeze.
- Infested logs can be treated by **peeling the bark**, **burning or scorching in a pile — preferably with snow on the ground**, **burying under at least eight inches of soil**, or **chipping**. After emergence, the wood can be used without threat to other trees.
- Under outbreak conditions, enough beetles can emerge from one infested tree to kill at least two more the following year.

**Verified — C.R.S. 39-22-543 as amended (HB26-1289):** secondary treatment of woody fuels by **lopping and scattering, piling, chipping, removing from the site**, or prescribed burning is a qualifying wildfire mitigation measure for the state tax credit, provided it meets or exceeds CSFS standards. From tax year 2027 the credit also covers **"infestation mitigation measures"** — thinning of woody vegetation at risk of, or killed by, mountain pine beetle or spruce beetle. Not mentioned in the post; see below.

**Corrected during research:**

- **"Thinning and slash treatment should only occur from October through April, as fresh cuts during beetle season attract beetles."** This is returned confidently by search and reads as CSFS guidance. The CSFS mountain pine beetle fact sheet was read in full and **contains no such window**. The only April–July dates in it refer to **preventive insecticide spraying of high-value trees**, which is a different operation entirely. The claim may well be sound advice from somewhere legitimate, but it could not be traced, and the nearest source carrying it was a commercial pine-beetle services site. **Not used.**
- Related: an early draft attributed the "October through April" rule to CSFS in the body copy. That is precisely the failure mode this project has been caught by before — a plausible specific hardening into a cited fact through repetition.

**Deliberately not used:**

- **The 2027 beetle-thinning tax credit.** It fits this topic better than any other, but it does not begin until tax year 2027 and post 2 already carries the credit. Folding it in here would make a practical post into a second funding post. **Flagged as the natural follow-up for a January or February slot**, when tax season makes it live.
- **Migratory Bird Treaty Act nesting restrictions** as a reason to cut in winter. Frequently cited in tree-work marketing; not researched here, and the federal position on incidental take has moved more than once. Would need checking before use.
- **Any claim about firewood value.** Softwood off a Front Range mitigation job is low-value, and implying a homeowner will recoup money on rounds would be overselling.

**Unresolved:**

- **Whether CSFS or CSU publishes a defensible cutting-season window anywhere.** Worth one direct question to a CSFS district forester — it would turn this post's central argument from inference into a citation, and it is a question a forester will enjoy answering.
- **Local slash-disposal options and fees** for the phase 1 target geography beyond the JeffCo SLASH sites already in the research file. The four-options paragraph would be more useful with a concrete local destination attached.
- **Whether chipping on site satisfies the CSFS standard** referenced by the tax credit statute. The statute names chipping as a qualifying secondary treatment; what the standard requires in practice was not established.
