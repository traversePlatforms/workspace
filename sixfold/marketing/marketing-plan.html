Marketing plan, August 2026 through May 2027

Prepared 2026-08-18 Horizon 10 months, three phases Source Phase 1 market research + name clearance screen

Draft. Nothing in this plan has been published, posted, or scheduled.

Every competitor sells the work. Nobody sells the money.

Four separate parties will pay for fire mitigation in Colorado, and the state's own auditors have concluded homeowners do not know it. The wildfire mitigation tax credit drew roughly 450 claims in the entire state in tax year 2023 — against more than a million buildings in the wildland-urban interface. That gap is the whole plan.

State tax credit $2,000

100% of costs up to $1,000 today; $2,000 and refundable from tax year 2027, income limit $300k AGI, extended through 2030. No application, no list, statewide.

County cost-share $25,000

Douglas County pays 50% up to $25,000, on verified completion. Requires two bids from the county's approved contractor list. That list is a gate, not a marketing task.

State FRWRM grants $7.04M

Open now, deadline 8 Oct 2026. Contractors cannot apply — HOAs, fire districts, nonprofits and utilities can. Sixfold is the contractor they hire.

Insurance HB25‑1182

In effect since 1 July 2026. Insurers must disclose each property's wildfire risk score annually and discount documented mitigation. Score notices are landing now.

01

Situation and positioning

Where Sixfold actually stands

Sixfold is an LLC with a bank account, a logo in two directions, and tools that can fell, grind, pile, chip, cut and haul. It has no insurance, no workers comp, no certifications, no portfolio, no reviews, and no website. It sits inside the largest wildfire-exposed suburban market in Colorado — about 4.5 million acres of wildland-urban interface holding more than a million buildings — against eight named competitors who all lead with credentials Sixfold does not yet have.

Competing on credentials is a fight Sixfold loses this year and possibly next. So don't take it.

The positioning

Sixfold is the fire mitigation company that gets the homeowner paid.

Not "we do defensible space" — everyone does defensible space. The offer is: we will show you the four places this money can come from, tell you which ones you qualify for, and do the work to the standard those programs require.

Why this beats a credentials strategy right now

A new company cannot manufacture a ten-year portfolio. It can manufacture the most useful conversation in the market inside a week. The funding knowledge is public, checkable, and nobody is using it as a sales motion — and it happens to be legible proof of competence. A contractor who can explain the credit's income limits and the county's two-bid requirement is obviously not a truck-and-a-chainsaw operation.

It also compounds into the credentials strategy rather than competing with it. Every funding channel has a paperwork gate: county lists want insurance and workers comp, cost-share wants a scope of work and a site map, insurance discounts want documentation of what was cut and where. Building the business to satisfy those gates is building the credentials.

Hard limits on copy — not preferences

  • No "licensed and insured" on anything — no flyer, site, door hanger or truck — until general liability and workers comp are in force. Fastest way to turn a marketing asset into a liability.
  • Do not advertise the Douglas County cost-share until Sixfold is on the approved contractor list. Pointing a homeowner at money they can only claim with two bids from a list Sixfold isn't on produces a dead lead and a bad first impression.
  • No claim of certification, accreditation or NFPA/Firewise standing that hasn't been verified as held. Competitors lead with this language; borrowing it is the one mistake that ends a new company's credibility permanently.
  • No aeration sector specifics. The mark is built as a parent brand and Sixfold is the entity that will carry the aeration business, but which kind is still unestablished. Fire is the only line described.
  • Funding language stays descriptive, never advisory. "The credit covers 100% of costs up to $1,000, rising to $2,000 and refundable for tax year 2027" is a fact with a source. "You'll get $2,000 back" is tax advice from a tree company. Every funding claim ships with its source and a line pointing to the homeowner's accountant or carrier.

What honestly cannot be sold yet

Mastication at scale. There is no masticator or mulching head in the equipment list, and Bear Claw's entire published product ladder is built on a forestry mulching skid steer. Sixfold sells handwork and chipping — genuinely the right product for Zone 1 and Zone 2 work close to a house, where machines struggle and homeowners are nervous. But it is a real gap on open acreage, and quoting large acre jobs against mulching competitors will lose on price. Pitch and price accordingly; don't pretend the gap isn't there.

02

The unfair advantage

Nobody in fire mitigation canvasses

Brandon already runs field canvassing infrastructure — recruiting, onboarding, training and managing door-to-door teams. In fire mitigation, nobody canvasses. The eight operators found in research are assessment-led, referral-led or credential-led; their pipeline is inbound. That matters more here than in most trades, for three reasons.

The product is only visible in the yard

A homeowner cannot judge their own fuel load. Standing in the driveway pointing at the juniper against the south wall, the dead limb over the roofline, and the twelve feet of grass to the fence line converts an abstract worry into a specific list. That conversation cannot happen over a search ad.

The trigger event arrives by mail with no vendor attached

Since 1 July 2026, insurers must send each Colorado policyholder an annual notice with their wildfire risk score and available mitigation discounts. Those notices are landing now. A homeowner holding one has been told they have a problem and given nobody to call. A canvasser on that street the same month is the only person answering a question the mail just asked.

Risk is geographic, and so is canvassing

Wildfire risk maps to specific streets — the ones backing onto open space, the foothill subdivisions, the drainages. Paid search cannot buy a street. Doors can walk one.

Three cautions — this advantage is also the plan's biggest exposure

  • Sixfold and Surge are separate companies. Using Surge's people or infrastructure for Sixfold's revenue is a related-party arrangement with tax and liability implications, and the shared address makes it visible. Structure it deliberately — Sixfold contracting Surge at an arm's-length rate, or reps engaged by Sixfold directly — before the first door is knocked. Flagged for Brandon and whoever does the books; not a marketing decision.
  • Solicitation rules vary by jurisdiction. Several Front Range municipalities require a solicitor's licence and set hours; subdivisions and HOAs post no-soliciting rules enforced socially even where not legally binding. Not verified in research — confirm per municipality. Getting run off a neighbourhood in month one costs more than the permit.
  • The register is "friendly neighbourhood," and canvassing wrecks that fast. No urgency theatre, no fear selling, no "your insurance is going to drop you." The homeowner already has the fear — the notice supplied it. Sixfold's job is to be the calm one with the answer.

The canvass motion

A script shape to be tested against real doors, not assumed:

  • Open on the change, not the sale — the insurance law, the tax credit, or the county programme, whichever applies to that street.
  • Hand over the one-pager. Physical, plain, checkable, sources printed on it. It survives the door closing, which no pitch does.
  • Ask for the walk, not the contract. "Would it help if I walked the property and told you what a program would want cut?" A free assessment is a low-friction yes — and it is also the scope of work a cost-share application requires. One visit produces the lead, the quote and the paperwork.
  • Log every door — address, outcome, risk observations, insurance-notice status. That log becomes the spring campaign's target list.

Two lead pools the canvass can work now

Jefferson County SLASH sites — weekends, 15 May – 25 Oct 2026, $24 per 6-cubic-yard load. Everyone in that queue has self-identified as having more slash than they can handle and is paying to solve it. Confirm site rules on contractor presence before showing up.

HOAs and fire protection districts in Douglas and Jefferson counties — the eligible FRWRM applicants, with a live 8 October 2026 deadline. A contractor who helps assemble an application is the contractor that application names.

03

The seasonal frame

Demand and workability run opposite

This is the structural fact the whole calendar hangs on. Demand peaks in spring, as fire season approaches and the yard becomes visible again. The work is easiest and safest in the dormant season — late fall through early spring — when sap is down, ground is firm, there's no nesting activity and no burn-restriction anxiety. The busy months are the wrong months to be selling, and the good months are the ones when nobody calls.

Two ways to use that rather than fight it. Sell the quiet season as the better season, with reasons — dormant work is genuinely better for the property, and a new company with open capacity can price it that way honestly. And treat the quiet months as build months: November through February is when insurance gets bought, county lists get joined, the site gets built, reviews get collected and the canvass gets trained.

The one seasonal trap

Spring is also when every competitor is booked and every homeowner wants it done yesterday. A new company with no crew depth can take work it cannot deliver on time — and the reviews it spent all winter earning are exactly what it loses. Capacity, not leads, is the spring constraint. Phase 3 sets a booking limit rather than a lead target.

04

Phase 1 — Prove it

Now through end of October 2026 · roughly ten weeks

Goal

Finish the year with evidence, not revenue

Completed jobs, photographs, reviews, verified programme knowledge, and the gates opened. There is a hard sequencing reality: almost nothing scales until insurance exists, so the unblocking tasks come first even though they aren't marketing.

Unblock — week 1–2, in this order

  1. Get real insurance quotes. Indicative Colorado figures: GL $900–$2,600/yr, workers comp $7–$13 per $100 of payroll, $1M/$2M typical minimum, full package $3,000–$10,000. These are broker-site figures — get actual quotes. Colorado has a strict one-employee workers comp threshold.
  2. Phone the Douglas County Building Division about the approved contractor list. The county site returns HTTP 403 to automated fetch, so requirements cannot be desk-researched. Ask what the list requires, when the next window opens, and how long listing takes. The highest-leverage twenty minutes available.
  3. Register sixfoldland.com — DONE, and it is SixfoldColorado.com, owned since 2026-08-21 with Contact@SixfoldColorado.com on it. sixfoldland.com was never registered. Hosting is the remaining step and print now depends on it — the URL went onto the customer-facing sheets on 2026-09-18.
  4. Book the trademark attorney call — USPTO classes 37 and 44, with Sixfold AI, Inc. as the known conflict to raise. Cheap now, expensive after a truck is wrapped.
  5. Establish what Sixfold actually holds in licensing and certification, and what the market treats as table stakes. This determines what the copy is allowed to say.

Build the one asset that does the work — week 2–3

The funding one-pager. Physical first, a site page later. Plain layout, no salesmanship, four sources with citations printed on it: the state credit table by tax year, the county cost-share terms, the insurance disclosure law, and what documentation each one wants — with a line pointing the reader to their accountant and their carrier.

This is the campaign; everything else distributes it. It works at a door, at a SLASH site, in an HOA meeting, and attached to a quote — and it is the only asset that makes a brand-new company look like the most informed operator on the street.

Sell — weeks 3–10

  • Target the first ten jobs deliberately close to home — Monument outward into the Rampart Range and southern Douglas foothills. Short mobilisation keeps a cheap launch job survivable.
  • Run the launch offer — version A, B or the middle path, still Brandon's call. Whichever it is, the offer is a known, bounded cost of buying a portfolio, and it ends when the ten are done.
  • Work the SLASH queue through 25 October, weekends, site rules confirmed first.
  • Approach HOAs and fire districts before 8 October on FRWRM. Even a failed application builds the relationship that matters when awards land in March.
  • Sell the disposal choice explicitly. Haul-off vs. leave on site vs. chip vs. rounds is the biggest swing in any quote. Competitors bury it in an assumption; pricing each option is both honest and a visibly better buying experience.

Harvest — every single job, no exceptions

This is the part that gets skipped when the crew is tired, and it is the entire point of phase 1.

  • Photographs: same angle, before and after. Fixed positions, from the property line and from the house, wide enough to read the whole zone. This is the portfolio — and by winter it is also the proof-of-mitigation documentation an insurance discount asks for.
  • A written scope on every job — what was cut, where, to what spacing, what happened to the material. It becomes the county's scope-of-work format, the insurer's documentation, and the homeowner's tax-credit receipt trail. One document, three uses.
  • Ask for the review at completion, on site, phone in hand. Not by email later. Ten reviews from ten jobs is achievable; asking a month later gets three.
  • Log every property, worked or not, into the canvass database.
  • No customer named without written permission. A photograph of someone's house is identifiable, and their address is private by default.

Phase 1 done looks like: insurance quoted and bought · DougCo list requirements known and application in · domain registered, attorney consulted · ten jobs completed, photographed, scoped and reviewed · one-pager in circulation · a property log with real addresses in it · zero published claims that cannot be substantiated.

05

Phase 2 — Build the machine

November 2026 – February 2027 · the quiet quarter

Goal

Turn ten jobs into a company that can catch spring

This is where the compounding happens. Nothing here is urgent, which is exactly why it gets dropped — and dropping it means arriving at April with leads and no machine.

Close the gates

  • Insurance in force — GL and workers comp. Everything downstream unlocks, including the word "insured," which becomes usable the day it is true and not before.
  • Get on every county list that exists. Douglas is the priority, but the same work applies to any county running a cost-share or approved-contractor programme. Each list is a referral channel with no per-lead cost.
  • Certification. Establish what the market's table stakes actually are — competitors lead with certified sawyers and NFPA 1144 / Firewise language — and get the one that opens the most doors. This is the credential gap closing.
  • Decide the mastication question — rent, subcontract, or stay handwork-only. It determines whether Sixfold can quote acreage in spring or keeps declining it. A rental rate on a mulching head is a cheaper answer than a purchase, and worth pricing this quarter.

Build the site at SixfoldColorado.com

Small and useful beats broad:

  • The funding explainer as the centrepiece — the page that earns links, gets shared in HOA email threads, and answers a search nobody else is answering.
  • Before/after galleries from phase 1, organised by job type.
  • Published pricing, or at least published ranges. Almost nobody in the market does this; Bear Claw is the exception, and their card is resort-county pricing that doesn't transfer to the Front Range. Plain numbers are a real differentiator against a field of "call for a quote." Base them on Sixfold's own ten completed jobs — actual costs, actual hours — not on the CSFS 2020 table, which is six years stale, excludes 35–51% overhead, and has no verified escalation index applied.
  • Service area stated as where Sixfold works, never as a limit. One office is not a service area.
  • Contact that works on a phone, because it will be opened at a door.

Build the reviews engine

Reviews are the credential a new company can buy with effort rather than years. Make asking systematic — part of job closeout, not a favour someone remembers. Google Business Profile first, since that's where a homeowner checking a contractor actually looks.

Sell the dormant season

The winter offer, honestly argued: better for the property, better conditions, open capacity, empty calendar. Target the phase 1 property log first — the people who got a walk and a one-pager and said "not yet."

Land the Q1 tax-credit push

January through April is when the credit stops being abstract. Two audiences:

  • Homeowners who did work in 2026 — a short "here's how to claim it, here's your scope document, here's the form" note to every phase 1 customer. Costs nothing, generates referrals, and is the most concrete possible demonstration of the positioning.
  • Homeowners considering 2027 work — tax year 2027 is when the credit hits $2,000, becomes refundable, raises the limit to $300k AGI, and explicitly adds thinning woody vegetation. The strongest version of the story, and it applies to work booked in spring.

Train the canvass

Recruit and train against the phase 1 door log, with the script that survived contact, permits confirmed per municipality. The goal is that the canvass is ready in February, not starting in April.

06

Phase 3 — The spring push

March – May 2027

Goal

Convert the machine into booked, deliverable revenue

Without overselling capacity — the one failure mode that undoes both previous phases.

Four things converge

  1. Demand peaks as fire season approaches and yards become visible.
  2. Insurance renewals and annual score notices cycle again, with a year of documented mitigation now behind phase 1 and 2 customers.
  3. FRWRM awards are announced 31 March 2027. Grantees — HOAs, districts, nonprofits — have money and need contractors. Any relationship built the previous September pays here.
  4. The Douglas County window, if the anticipated late-2026 round slipped or a 2027 round opens on the April–May pattern the 2026 window followed. Being listed makes Sixfold one of the two bids applicants must obtain.

The motion

  • Canvass at scale, targeting logged streets first: properties walked but not sold, and neighbours of completed jobs. A finished job with visible before/after next door is the best canvassing asset in existence — work outward from it.
  • Lead with proof now, funding second. The order flips. Phase 1 and 2 bought photographs and reviews; in spring the funding explainer becomes the closer rather than the opener.
  • Book to a stated capacity limit and publish the lead time. "Next availability is three weeks" is more credible than instant availability, and it protects the reviews. When the calendar fills, stop selling and say why.
  • Convert grantees and HOAs on common-area and community chipping work — including Douglas County's up-to-$7,000 community chipping money, once listing allows it to be mentioned.

Phase 3 done looks like: a booked spring delivered on time · reviews still climbing · a repeatable canvass with known cost per lead and per job · at least one grant-funded or cost-share job completed · enough real cost data to price 2027–28 properly.

Metrics

Small and honest. Vanity metrics are the failure mode here.

Phase 1 — evidence

  • Jobs completed (target 10)
  • Before/after photo sets captured — should equal jobs completed, no exceptions
  • Reviews collected (target 8–10 from 10 jobs; asked on site, not by email)
  • DougCo list: requirements known → application submitted → listed
  • Insurance: quoted → bound
  • Properties logged in the canvass database

Phase 2 — machine

  • Reviews total and average rating
  • One-pagers distributed / assessments booked from them
  • County lists joined
  • Dormant-season jobs sold from the phase 1 property log
  • Site live with pricing based on real job costs
  • Phase 1 customers given their tax-credit paperwork (should be 100%)

Phase 3 — conversion

  • Doors knocked → assessments booked → jobs sold. These two conversion rates are the numbers the whole canvass thesis lives or dies on
  • Cost per lead and cost per job by channel
  • Booked lead time, and jobs delivered on the promised date
  • Grant-funded or cost-share jobs completed
  • Repeat and referral share of revenue

The number that matters most across all three phases: jobs delivered on the date promised. Everything else is recoverable.
