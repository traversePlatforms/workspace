## Block 1 — The state tax credit

**$1,000 this year. $2,000 next year, and refundable.**

Colorado's wildfire mitigation tax credit covers **100% of what you spend** on qualifying vegetation work, up to the cap. There is no application and no waiting list — it is claimed on your state income tax return.

- **Tax year 2026:** 100% of costs, up to **$1,000**
- **Tax year 2027:** 100% of costs, up to **$2,000**, and **refundable** — meaning you can receive it even if you owe no state tax. The income limit rises to $300,000 AGI.
- Extended through **tax year 2030**
- **Thinning woody vegetation now qualifies explicitly** — that is the core of this work
- Income limits apply. The credit covers vegetation work, **not** structural work like roofing, vents or siding.

*Source: Colorado General Assembly 2026 tax expenditure evaluation; Colorado Department of Revenue, tax.colorado.gov/wildfire-tax-benefits. Confirm your own eligibility with your accountant.*

## Block 2 — Your insurer now has to tell you, and has to discount it

**Since 1 July 2026, this is the law — not a courtesy.**

If your insurer uses a wildfire risk score to price, underwrite, non-renew or surcharge your policy, Colorado law now requires them to:

- **Tell you your property's wildfire risk score, in writing, every year**
- **Say what mitigation discounts are available** to you
- **Count the work you've actually done** — defensible space specifically — in how they score and price your property
- **Give you a discount for documented mitigation**, even if they have no formal mitigation model of their own
- **Let you appeal the score** if you think it's wrong

If a score notice has arrived in your mail this summer, that is why.

*Source: Colorado HB25-1182, leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb25-1182. Confirm what your own policy qualifies for with your carrier.*

## Block 3 — What the paperwork actually needs

The money is real. What stops people claiming it is documentation nobody told them to keep.

- **For the tax credit:** what you spent, and evidence the work was qualifying vegetation work.
- **For your insurer:** evidence of what was cut, where, and to what spacing — before and after.

**Every Sixfold job comes with both, whether or not you ask.** A written scope of exactly what was cut and where, and photographs from fixed positions before and after. One document set, and it serves your tax return and your carrier.

## Lines cut, with reasons

Kept here so the sheet can be lengthened later without re-researching.

- **"More than a million buildings in Colorado sit in the wildland-urban interface."** True and sourced (CSFS, 2023), but it argues the problem rather than the money, and the reader already believes they have a problem. Better suited to the web version.
- **"Only about 450 people in the entire state claimed this credit last year."** Excellent fact, sourced to the legislative evaluation. Cut from the sheet because at the door it invites "so why would I?" rather than "so I should." Works better in the web explainer, where the reader is already curious.
- **"A one-acre defensible space project runs $3,200 to $6,100."** Sourced (CSFS via the 2026 evaluation) and genuinely useful for setting expectations. Cut because putting a cost range on a handout before Sixfold has its own job costs invites a quote comparison Sixfold hasn't priced yet. Revisit in phase 2 with real numbers.
- **"Insurers are non-renewing policies across Colorado."** Directionally true and widely reported, but it is fear-selling and the plan bars it. The homeowner's own notice supplies the urgency.
- **Any reference to being licensed, insured, certified, or Firewise-accredited.** None of it is true yet.
- **A percentage figure for the insurance discount.** The law requires a discount; it does not set a rate, and no verified figure exists. Stating one would be invention.
- **The 2026 income limit for the tax credit.** The source table lists it as TBD. The sheet says "income limits apply" and names only the 2027 figure, which is published.
