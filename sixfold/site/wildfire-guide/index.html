Colorado wildfire · a homeowner's guide

Embers start most of the homes that burn.

Wind carries burning debris more than a mile ahead of a fire. It lands in your gutter, under your deck, in the mulch against your wall. That is the part you can do something about.

We can also come and walk your property.

How a house catches

Three mechanisms, and one of them does most of it.

Embers

Land on anything that will take a spark. The usual cause.

Flame

Reaches the wall when fuel sits within five feet of it.

Heat

Lights a wall with nothing touching it at all.

All three are real, which is why "clear the trees back" on its own is not a plan. The order below is the order that matters: the house first, then the ground around it, then the distances.

Start here, before anything outdoors

Seven places a house catches.

Work outward from the wall, not inward from the property line. Ask us about any of it.

  1. Class A roof. No wood or shake shingle.
  2. Clear gutters, roof and deck of needles more than once a year.
  3. 1/8-inch metal mesh on every vent. Standard rodent screen is 1/4 and lets embers through.
  4. Screen under decks and stilt foundations. Store nothing there.
  5. Tempered glass, two panes or more. Windows fail before the building does.
  6. Six inches of clearance between the ground and the bottom of the siding.
  7. Non-combustible fence within five feet. A fence on the house is part of the house.

Fibre cement, brick and stucco beat wood siding under radiant heat.

Why the mesh size is the whole point

An ember passes straight through a 1/4-inch grid and is stopped by a 1/8-inch one. Attic, eave, foundation, under-deck — every vent. This is the item people most often think they have already done.

Labelled stops most, not "stops": the source is explicit that 1/8-inch screening only minimises ember entry. Overstating a barrier is how someone stops checking it.

Firefighters may not be there. They engage only when it is safe for them to. Prepare the house to stand on its own.

Then work outward

Three zones, measured from the wall.

Build them around every structure — house, garage, shed, barn. Felling, thinning, spacing and clearing to these distances is what we do.

ZoneDistanceThe instruction
Zone 1 0–5 ft
strip it		 Rock, gravel or bare earth. No shrubs, mulch, firewood or deck storage. Rake needles within 5 ft of every deck and structure. Clear 10 ft around the chimney.
Zone 2 5–30 ft
thin it		 Grass to 4 inches. 10 ft between crowns — and if you leave small groups of two or three, 30 ft between those groups. Prune up 6–10 ft, or a third of the tree, whichever is less. Pull ladder fuels and ground juniper. Shrub clumps 2½× their mature height apart.
Zone 3 30–100 ft
open it		 6–10 ft of crown spacing, with real gaps between clumps so fire has to drop to the ground. Take the woody debris away rather than scattering it.

Zone 2, from the side

Crown spacing and pruning height are the two rules a number alone does not convey. The gap is measured between crowns, not trunks — and a shrub sitting under a pruned tree undoes the pruning, because it is a ladder back up into the canopy.

These figures are for ponderosa, Douglas-fir and mixed conifer below 9,500 ft. Piñon-juniper, lodgepole, spruce-fir, Gambel oak and aspen all differ — aspen should generally not be thinned at all. On slopes, increase every spacing.

Off the wall and out to the road

Two numbers do most of the work.

  • 30 ftFirewood, propane and slash — all of it at least that far from the house.
  • UphillStack firewood uphill of the house or level with it, never below — and never between trees or under the deck. Propane: level with the house where you can.
  • 10 ftClear flammable vegetation that far around woodpiles, propane tanks and gas meters.
  • Burning?Call the sheriff or your fire department first. Restrictions change constantly.
  • DrivewayThin 30 ft back from each side, house to main road. A house number at the road, noncombustible and reflective — a crew that cannot find you cannot defend you.

And for the part that wants a saw

Someone else will help pay for it.

Colorado's tax credit covers 100% of a contractor's invoice for qualifying vegetation work, up to a cap, and since July 2026 your insurer has to show you your wildfire risk score and what mitigation would do to it. Almost nobody knows either exists.
