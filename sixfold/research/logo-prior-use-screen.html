Sixfold · Preliminary screen · 19 Aug 2026

Has anyone else drawn this mark?

A search of the US trademark register and of thirteen Colorado operators in the same trade, whose logos were downloaded and inspected one by one.

The short answer

Nothing found resembles it. No mark in the trade combines flat geometric ascending triangles with a tree and roots, and no "Sixfold" trademark exists in the land or construction classes.

The nuance worth carrying into the room: the ingredients are generic, the arrangement is not. Mountains and conifers are among the most heavily worked elements in this sector — 11,430 registered figurative marks carry one or the other in the two relevant service classes alone. What nobody else is doing is drawing them as flat overlapping triangles stepping up in opacity. And nobody at all is drawing roots.

What this screen could not do

Stated up front, not buried

No reverse image search. That is the standard first tool for this question and I have no way to submit an image to one. It is the single largest gap here, and it is five minutes of work for anyone with a browser.

No USPTO design-code search. The USPTO's search is a JavaScript application with no reachable public API — confirmed against four candidate endpoints. That is the one search that would authoritatively answer "does someone own this," and it needs a person at a browser or an attorney.

This is a preliminary screen, not a clearance, and not legal advice.

The pattern across all ten that resolved

Every single one is a badge — circle, oval, hexagon or shield — holding either illustrative scenery or an animal-and-tool motif. Not one uses flat geometric shapes. Not one uses roots. The Sixfold mark does not look like this field, which answers the question asked and, separately, is an argument for the mark.

How crowded the category is

11,430Registered figurative marks with a mountain or conifer code, service classes 37 + 44, all offices
1,961Conifer-coded marks, US portion, all classes
577Mountain-coded marks, US portion, all classes

Treat the two US figures as a bad undercount. The same database reports only two mountain-coded marks in class 37 across the entire US register, which cannot be true — the USPTO classifies designs under its own scheme rather than the international one, and that data travels across incompletely. The all-offices number is the honest one, and it points one way: a mountain or a conifer, on its own, is close to useless as a distinguishing element in this trade.

Which parts of the mark are actually distinctive

Generic — do not rely on these

A conifer. Triangular peaks. A horizontal ground line. Each appears in thousands of registrations, and none of them distinguishes anything on its own.

Distinctive — this is the mark

Overlapping triangles stepping up in opacity as they ascend, and roots descending from one end of the ground line. Root imagery exists in logo design generally, but attached to a trunk — not hanging off a baseline under a mountain range. The two together turned up nothing.

What to do next

  1. Run a reverse image searchGoogle Images, TinEye or Bing Visual Search. Drop the PNG in. This covers the ground I could not, and it takes five minutes.
  2. Run a USPTO design-code searchCodes 05.01.01 (evergreen and conical trees) and 06.01.02 (mountains), crossed with classes 37 and 44, at tmsearch.uspto.gov. Trivial in a browser.
  3. Attorney review before anything physicalOnly if the mark is going onto vehicles, signage or apparel. The name screen already recommended this and the mark does not change it either way.
  4. Nothing here blocks proceedingDrafts, sheets and a website are fine. The exposure sits with durable physical application, not with digital work.
