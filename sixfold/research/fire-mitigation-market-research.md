# Fire Mitigation Market Research — Colorado

**For:** Sixfold LLC
**Prepared:** 2026-08-18
**Status:** Phase 1 research. Every figure traced to a primary source or named outlet; unresolved items listed at the bottom rather than guessed.

---

## Executive summary

Five things matter more than the rest:

1. **The demand is real and the state documents it.** About 4.5 million acres of Colorado is wildland-urban interface, holding more than 1 million buildings and roughly 2.5 million people — just under half the state's population. More than 1 million of those residents live where wildfire risk is moderate to very high. (CSFS, 2023, on end-of-2022 data.)

2. **The highest-value risk is suburban, not remote mountain.** Front Range plains and foothill counties — El Paso, Douglas, Jefferson, Larimer, Boulder, Teller, Park — carry the most property exposure. Sixfold is based on the Front Range. The work is close to home.

3. **Four separate parties will pay for this work, and most homeowners know about none of them.** County cost-share, state grants, a state income tax credit, and — new as of six weeks ago — insurance carriers. The tax credit drew roughly **450 claims in the entire state** in tax year 2023. The state's own evaluation concluded awareness is the problem. Whoever explains the money wins the job.

4. **Douglas County pays 50% up to $25,000 — but only to homeowners who submit two bids from the county's approved contractor list.** Getting Sixfold onto that list is a gate, not a marketing task. It is the single highest-leverage action on this document.

5. **Published competitor pricing is resort-county pricing.** The one Colorado firm publishing a rate card serves Winter Park, Pitkin, Eagle and Summit. Front Range pricing should not be set against it without adjustment.

### Where the risk concentrates

The Colorado Sun, reporting on First Street Foundation modeling, found the greatest wildfire *property* risk is not in the forested high country but in the Front Range's growing suburban plains counties — **El Paso, Douglas and Larimer**. Separate USFS research on fire transmission names **El Paso, Douglas and Jefferson** communities as carrying relatively more risk.

This is the important correction to the intuitive picture. Fire mitigation is not only a mountain-cabin business. The Marshall Fire burned suburban Boulder County in grass, not timber.

**Counties funded in the most recent state grant round (24 named):** Alamosa, Archuleta, Boulder, Chaffee, Conejo, Costilla, Douglas, El Paso, Garfield, Grand, Jackson, Jefferson, La Plata, Larimer, Las Animas, Mesa, Mineral, Montezuma, Park, Rio Grande, Routt, Saguache, Teller. Where the state puts money is a usable proxy for where the work is.

## 2. Who pays — the four funding channels

This section should drive the marketing plan. The work is expensive; the reason homeowners stall is money; and there is more money available than they know.

### Channel A — County cost-share

**Douglas County Wildfire Mitigation Cost-Share Program**
- 50% match to homeowners, **up to $25,000**
- Up to **$50,000** for community projects
- Up to **$7,000** for community chipping or slash pickup
- 2026 application window ran 13 April – 13 May; a **second window is anticipated in late 2026**
- Requires a detailed scope of work, a site map, and **two contractor estimates from the county's approved contractor list**
- Paid as reimbursement after county staff verify completion and the homeowner proves they paid their half

**Boulder County — Wildfire Partners**
- Rebates up to **$500** per property for defensible space actions
- 2026 Multi-Family Dwelling rebate: up to **$500 per unit**; applications closed 24 July 2026

**Jefferson County — SLASH (Sustainable Lands and Safer Homes)**
- Seasonal slash collection sites, open to non-residents, fee-based
- Not a cash program, but it is where JeffCo homeowners with a slash problem physically go. That is a lead pool standing in a line.

### Channel B — State grants

**Forest Restoration & Wildfire Risk Mitigation (FRWRM), administered by CSFS**
- Most recent award: **$8.5 million across 35 projects** (from 73 eligible applications), leveraging to **$17.6 million** with match
- Program lifetime: **312 projects, more than 30,000 acres**
- Currently open: **$7.04 million**, deadline **8 October 2026, 5:00 p.m.**, awards announced 31 March 2027
- State covers 50% or 75% depending on whether the area is designated as having fewer economic resources; the applicant matches the rest, cash or in-kind
- **Eligible applicants are community groups, fire protection districts, tribes, utilities and non-profits — not individual contractors.** Sixfold cannot apply. Sixfold can be the contractor a grantee hires, and can help an HOA or fire district assemble an application.
- Preference for projects using accredited Colorado Youth or Veterans Corps labor, protecting watersheds or infrastructure, or leveraging other funding

**COSWAP (Colorado Strategic Wildfire Action Program)** — separate DNR program funding crew time and workforce development, including SWIFT inmate crews and youth corps. Relevant as both a competitor for simple work and a possible partner.

### Channel C — The state income tax credit

The most underexploited thing in this entire file.

Per the Colorado General Assembly's 2026 tax expenditure evaluation:

| Tax year | Income limit | Credit % of costs | Max credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 | $120,000 FTI | 25% | $625 |
| 2024 | $126,300 FTI | 25% | $625 |
| 2025 | $129,200 FTI | 100% | $1,000 |
| 2026 | TBD | 100% | $1,000 |
| 2027 | $300,000 federal AGI | 100% | **$2,000, refundable** |

Additional findings from that evaluation:
- **About 450 claims statewide in tax year 2023.** Total revenue impact: $198,158.
- The evaluation's own conclusion: the credit "has been underutilized," and "awareness of the credit is low, with many reporting they had not heard about the credit prior to our outreach."
- HB26-1289 extended the credit through **tax year 2030**, made it refundable from 2027, raised the cap to $2,000, raised the income limit to $300,000 AGI, and **expanded eligibility to include thinning woody vegetation** — precisely Sixfold's core service.
- The credit cannot be claimed for structural work (roofing, vents, siding). It covers the vegetation work.
- The older WUI-only *deduction* ended after tax year 2024; from 2025 only the credit is available.

**What this means commercially:** a homeowner doing a $6,000 defensible space project in 2027 can get $2,000 back as a refundable credit, on top of a possible 50% county match. Almost nobody has been told this. A one-page explainer walking a homeowner through stacking county cost-share and the tax credit is worth more than any ad.

### Channel D — Insurance (new, and the strongest current hook)

**HB25-1182 took effect 1 July 2026 — six weeks ago.** Any insurer using a wildfire risk model or score to underwrite, price, non-renew or surcharge a Colorado homeowners policy must now:
- send an annual written notice stating the property's wildfire risk score and the mitigation discounts available
- incorporate parcel-level mitigation — defensible space specifically — into scoring and pricing
- provide a discount for documented mitigation even where the insurer has no formal mitigation model
- allow the policyholder to appeal the score

Homeowners across Colorado are receiving score notices for the first time right now, some alongside non-renewals. That is a demand event with a date on it, and it converts mitigation from "should do someday" into "this changes my premium."

**Read on the field:**

- Almost nobody publishes prices. Bear Claw is the exception. That is an opening — plain published pricing is a differentiator in a market of "call for a quote."
- The credible operators lead with **credentials** (firefighters, veterans, NFPA/Firewise standards) rather than equipment. Credentialing is the cost of entry for trust.
- Several are arborists or land-clearing firms that added fire mitigation as a line item. A company that is *only* fire mitigation reads more specialist.
- Nobody found so far leads with **the money** — the county match, the tax credit, the insurance discount. That lane looks open.

## 4. What the work actually costs

Primary source: **CSFS 2020 Colorado Forest Action Plan, Appendix 7**, average costs by forest cover type and CSFS area. The Front Range is the **Northeast (NE)** area.

**Northeast area, cost per acre (2020 dollars):**

| Forest type | Range | Average |
|---|---|---|
| Ponderosa pine | $1,200 – $3,500 | $2,300 |
| Mixed conifer | $1,400 – $3,000 | $2,000 |
| Oak shrubland (incl. ponderosa-oak mix) | $800 basic mastication – $2,875 handwork | $2,200 |
| Lodgepole pine | $700 – $4,000 | $1,700 (no commercial value) |
| Riparian | $2,500 – $4,700 | $3,600 |
| Aspen / hardwood | $1,200 – $1,300 | $1,250 |

CSFS's own notes on what moves the number: scope, project acreage, travel and mobilization distance, handwork vs. machine, harvesting-and-hauling vs. mastication only, product utilization requirements, slope and terrain, complexity, crew and equipment, and **work around homes** — which CSFS flags explicitly: "All costs increase in wildland-urban interface (WUI)."

**Two critical caveats:**

1. **These are 2020 figures.** Six years old, and they must be escalated before they touch a quote or published copy. I have not applied an escalation factor because I do not have a verified index to apply — flagged as unresolved below.
2. **They exclude overhead.** CSFS states implementation "averages 35% for overhead/administration but can be as high as 51%." That is on top of the numbers above.

**Per-home benchmark:** the state's tax credit evaluation, citing CSFS data, puts a one-acre defensible space project around a home at **$3,200 to $6,100 total**. That is the number to price a residential job against, and it is the most recent CSFS-sourced figure found.

**Firewood as a secondary line:** Denver / Front Range delivered cord prices run roughly $250–$500 depending on species and dryness, clustering around $300–$350 for mixed. Softwood — most of what comes off a Front Range mitigation job — sits at the low end, $150–$300. This is margin recovery on wood already on the truck, not a business. Treat it that way.

## 5. What Sixfold can sell today

Mapping the stated equipment and capability to sellable service lines:

| Capability | Service line | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cut down trees | Thinning, hazard tree and standing dead removal | Core of every defensible space job |
| Flatten stumps | Stump grinding | Usually an upsell; often what the homeowner actually cares about visually |
| Make slash piles | Fuels reduction, lop-and-scatter, pile construction | Cheapest disposal method; sets the low price point |
| Chip the wood | On-site chipping | Also maps to county community-chipping money (DougCo: up to $7,000) |
| Create rounds | Firewood rounds | Secondary revenue; low margin on softwood |
| Haul off or leave | Disposal choice | The biggest swing factor in a quote — sell it as a choice, not an assumption |

**Sellable now:** Zone 1 and Zone 2 defensible space, hazard tree removal, ladder fuel reduction, understory thinning, slash disposal, community chipping days, HOA common-area work.

**Not sellable yet:** anything requiring mastication at scale — no masticator or mulching head in the stated equipment list. Bear Claw's entire product ladder is built on a forestry mulching skid steer. That is the main equipment gap between Sixfold and a full-service competitor.

## 6. Where to work

Recommended priority, based on the risk data and the funding data together:

**Tier 1 — Douglas County.** Highest property risk in the state per First Street modeling, a $25,000-per-homeowner cost-share program with a second 2026 window coming, and an approved-contractor list that functions as a referral channel. Close to Monument.

**Tier 2 — Jefferson County.** High risk, dense foothills WUI, established slash infrastructure, mountain communities (Evergreen, Conifer, the Bailey corridor) that already understand mitigation. Also where Forestwerx and Splintered Forest are strongest — this is the contested ground.

**Tier 3 — El Paso County.** Largest raw property exposure. Colorado Springs Fire Department runs a neighborhood chipping program and a public mitigation tax credit push, so the market is educated. Further from base; a second-phase target.

**Tier 4 — Boulder, Larimer, Teller, Park.** Real risk and real money, but either well-served already (Boulder's Wildfire Partners is the most mature program in the state) or a long drive.

**On "underserved":** I could not find a defensible answer to which areas are underserved by contractors. Contractor-density data by county does not appear to be published, and inferring it from search result counts is exactly how a wrong number becomes a confident one. What can be said: the state funded projects in 24 counties last round, and the operators found here cluster heavily in Jefferson, Evergreen, Colorado Springs and the resort counties. The San Luis Valley counties (Alamosa, Conejos, Costilla, Saguache, Rio Grande, Mineral) received grant money and had no operator surface in this search — a signal worth checking directly, not a conclusion.

## 7. Strategic read

**Position Sixfold as the company that gets the homeowner paid.**

Every competitor sells the work. Nobody sells the funding. The research supports that gap being real and large:
- 450 tax credit claims statewide, against more than 1 million buildings in the WUI
- The state's own auditors concluding awareness is the binding constraint
- A brand new insurance disclosure law putting risk scores in mailboxes right now
- A county writing $25,000 checks that requires two bids to access

That positioning also solves the new-company problem. Sixfold has no track record, no portfolio and no reviews. It cannot win on credentials against a veteran-owned outfit with certified sawyers. It can win on being the one that shows up with the paperwork, the county list, the tax form and a clear number.

**Immediate action items, in order:**

1. **Apply to the Douglas County approved contractor list** before the late-2026 window opens. Homeowners need two bids from that list; being on it puts Sixfold in front of every applicant. Expect to need proof of liability insurance, workers comp, and possibly certification.
2. Do the same for every other county running cost-share or an approved list.
3. Get whatever certification the market treats as table stakes. Competitors lead with NFPA 1144 / Firewise language and certified sawyers. Establish what Sixfold actually holds.
4. Introduce Sixfold to fire protection districts and HOAs in Douglas and Jefferson counties — they are the eligible FRWRM applicants, and the 8 October 2026 deadline is live.
5. Build the funding explainer. One page, plain, checkable.

## 8. Fact-check log

**Verified against primary or first-party sources:**
- WUI acreage, buildings, population figures — CSFS press release, 28 Sep 2023, on end-of-2022 CO-WRA data
- Treatment cost per acre by forest type and region — CSFS 2020 Forest Action Plan Appendix 7, extracted from the source PDF
- 35% / 51% overhead figure — same CSFS appendix, direct quote
- Tax credit percentages, caps, income limits, tax years, refundability, claim counts, revenue impact, and the underutilisation finding — Colorado General Assembly 2026 tax expenditure evaluation (2026-TE4), extracted from the source PDF
- FRWRM award totals, project counts, open funding, deadline, eligibility and match — CSFS announcements, 31 Mar 2026 and 3 Aug 2026
- Bear Claw tier pricing and service area — Bear Claw's own pricing page
- HB25-1182 requirements and 1 July 2026 effective date — Colorado House Democrats joint release and the bill page
- $3,200–$6,100 one-acre defensible space cost — CSFS data as cited in the 2026 legislative evaluation

**Corrected during research:**
- **"CSFS says mitigation averages $1,700 per acre statewide."** Repeated across several SEO and consulting blogs, attributed to CSFS. The actual CSFS source gives no single statewide average — it gives per-forest-type, per-region ranges. $1,700 is the Northeast-area *lodgepole pine* average specifically. The statewide framing is aggregator drift. **Not used.** The real table is in §4.
- **Tax credit terms.** Search results simultaneously reported "25%, up to $2,500" and "100%, $1,000." Both were partly wrong. The $2,500 figure does not appear in the current statute at all. The legislative evaluation's year-by-year table (§2, Channel C) is authoritative and supersedes both.
- **Colorado Sun county property counts.** The summary returned garbled per-county property figures (El Paso "32,000" against "more than 80% of homes"). The article itself returned HTTP 403 and could not be read directly. **Directional finding used** — that suburban plains counties carry the greatest property risk — **specific property counts omitted.**

**Unresolved / needs a decision or a direct call:**
- **Escalation factor for the 2020 CSFS cost table.** Six years stale. Needs a verified index before those numbers inform a price list.
- **Douglas County approved contractor list requirements.** The county site returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch on three different URLs. Requires a phone call to the Building Division.
- **Whether the 2025 CO-WRA data refresh has landed.** The dataset updates every three years and end-of-2022 data is due for replacement.
- **Contractor density by county.** No published source found. The "underserved areas" question cannot be answered from desk research alone.
- **Colorado Springs and El Paso County program details** — not yet pulled.
- **What Sixfold actually holds** in insurance, licensing and certification. Determines which channels are open on day one.
