Water Restrictions 2026

What the crew needs to know before promising anything about a lawn. Organised by the towns we actually work, not by which agency wrote the rule. Bold lines are for the door. Sources are for the office.

Researched 2026-09-30 Base Monument, CO Draft 1 — not customer-facing

⚑ This document goes stale

Drought stages move without warning. Denver Water went from normal summer rules to a total lawn-watering ban in about five weeks. Nothing here should be quoted to a customer as current without checking the provider's own page that week. Every provider is linked at the foot of this page.

The one thing that shapes everything else

Aeration does not need water. Overseeding does.

Core aeration is a machine pass. It works on moist ground, and a customer under the tightest restrictions in our territory can still legally water enough to soften a lawn before we arrive. Our lead service is not threatened by any of this.

Overseeding is the exposed half. Seed needs light, frequent watering to germinate — more often than most providers now allow by sprinkler. A customer allowed to water two or three days a week is unlikely to get a good take from an overseed, and we do not water and do not guarantee germination.

So the risk is not legal. It is this: we take money for seed that does not come up, and the customer blames us. Everything below is about not walking into that.

The one most people get wrong: wells

⚑ A well is not a loophole

Most private wells on parcels under 35 acres permitted since 8 May 1972 are household-use-only. That means water for inside the house. It cannot legally be used to irrigate a lawn or garden at all — not two days a week, not at night, not ever. This is a condition of the well permit and has nothing to do with drought.

Parcels of 35 acres or more may carry full exempt-well uses, which can include up to one acre of lawn and garden.

What this means at the door: on a rural property, “I'm on a well so restrictions don't apply to me” may be exactly backwards. Ask what the well permit allows before quoting an overseed. If the answer is household-use-only, the seed has no legal water, and we should be selling aeration, mowing or cleanup instead.

The permit is the owner's and so is the responsibility. But quoting an overseed to someone who cannot legally water it is a job that fails and a customer who is unhappy.

The closing tool nobody else is using

Colorado Springs Utilities issues establishment permits, and most lawn outfits never mention them. It is the single most useful thing in this document for selling an overseed in the Springs.

  • Allows watering more often than three days a week, and during normally banned daytime hours
  • 42 days for seeding projects (28 days for sod, flowers, shrubs, trees)
  • Only needed for sprinklers. Drip, watering cans and hose-end with a shut-off nozzle are allowed any time regardless
  • Apply online, or in person at the Conservation and Environmental Center, 2855 Mesa Rd
  • Customer gets a printable permit and can pick up a yard sign

⚑ Read this before promising a customer they can get one

For sod or non-native grass seed, they must submit receipts for either 4 cubic yards per 1,000 sq ft of approved soil amendment, mixed into the soil, or a soil test from a professional laboratory.

On a ¾-acre lawn the amendment option is about 131 cubic yards. That does not happen on an aeration job. The soil test is the realistic route, and it is not something we currently include or price.

For native grass there is no soil prep requirement at all — just a receipt showing the mix, which has to be on the utility's qualifying list. That makes seed choice a business decision, not just an agronomy one. A qualifying native mix turns the permit into a receipt. A non-native mix turns it into a soil test the customer has to go and buy.

Can Sixfold get the permit itself?

Almost certainly not in our own name. The utility's wording is “any residential or commercial water customer.” At a customer's house the water customer is the customer — we have no account at that address and nothing to attach a permit to. The permit belongs to the property, not to the contractor.

What we can realistically do is apply on their behalf, or walk them through it — which is the useful version anyway, because it costs us nothing and it is the part customers do not know about. Whether the utility formally allows a contractor to file for a customer is not confirmed. The state landscape trade association publishes a contractor-facing video on these permits, which suggests contractors handle them routinely, but the video could not be reviewed and the utility's own document says nothing either way.

One call settles it: ESTP@csu.org, or the Conservation and Environmental Center, 2855 Mesa Rd.

Does it cost money?

No fee appears anywhere in the utility's own document. Both published copies of the establishment permit PDF were fetched and they are byte-identical — neither mentions a fee, a charge, or a cost.

A secondary site said $50. That figure is not corroborated by the utility and should not be repeated to a customer. Ask on the same phone call.

⚑ It is not a licence, and it is not per county

Three things that are easy to run together, and are not the same:

ThingWhat it isScope

The unit is the water provider, not the county. El Paso County alone contains Colorado Springs Utilities, Town of Monument, Woodmoor, Donala, Triview and Palmer Lake — six providers, six sets of rules, in one county. A permit from one is worth nothing at an address served by another.

What to say, and what not to

SayDo not say
“Aeration works fine under the current restrictions.” True everywhere in our territory. Anything that sounds like a promise the seed will come up. We do not water and do not guarantee germination — that is in the agreement, and it needs to match what we say at the door.
“Which water provider are you on?” Ask it on every lawn walk. One line, and it changes what we should sell. A specific watering schedule for a provider without checking it that week.
“If you're on Colorado Springs Utilities there's a permit that lets you water more often while new seed comes in. I can tell you how to get it.” “You're on a well so the restrictions don't apply.” Often the opposite.
“Water the lawn a day or two before we come out.” Now written into the agreements. A price for the establishment permit. We have not confirmed there is one.

What is not settled

Named here rather than quietly filled in.

  1. Colorado Springs' current stage was last confirmed in May. Utilities entered Water Shortage Preparation in March and the Gazette reported in April that they were emphasising caution rather than new restrictions. Denver escalated hard in late August and no September source for Colorado Springs could be found. The three-day figure is what our quotes and ads depend on. One phone call settles it.
  2. Donala, Triview and Palmer Lake were not pinned down. All three serve parts of Monument and Gleneagle. Donala draws from the depleting Denver Basin aquifer, which is the profile most likely to restrict.
  3. Three things about permits, all answerable in two phone calls. (a) Whether Colorado Springs lets a contractor file on a customer's behalf — ESTP@csu.org. (b) Whether there is a fee at all; a secondary source said $50 and both copies of the utility's own PDF are silent, so do not quote a price. (c) Whether Castle Rock's Registered Landscape Professional programme is required of us to work there, and what it costs — 720-733-2306.
  4. Whether the other six providers have an establishment scheme at all. Woodland Park, Town of Monument, Woodmoor, Donala, Triview and Palmer Lake were not checked. Do not assume.
  5. Whether mid-October is too late to seed at all. Cool-season overseeding is normally a late-summer job. If it is past the window, that is a bigger problem than the watering rules, and dormant seeding — seed down late, germinates in spring — may be the honest version. Not researched.
  6. Our live Meta ad says nothing about water. “Fall Aeration 2026” is running at $50/day and is aeration-led, which is the safe half. Worth a read-through now that this is known.

⚑ A sourcing note worth keeping

Two sites — water-restrictions.com and lawnbyseason.com — confidently asserted that Colorado Springs is under “Stage 1 Mandatory, twice per week, odd Monday/Wednesday, even Tuesday/Thursday.” That is Denver and Aurora's rule, misattributed to Colorado Springs. Both were blocked and the figures re-taken from csu.org and CPR.

This is the same failure that set the research standard in the house rules: content farms recycling confident, wrong specifics. If a watering schedule cannot be found on the provider's own site, it does not go in a quote.
