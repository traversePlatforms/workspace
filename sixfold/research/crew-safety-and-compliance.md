## The one finding that shapes everything else

**Sixfold's tree work is NOT covered by OSHA's logging standard.** It is covered by OSHA's
**General Industry** rules (29 CFR 1910) plus the **General Duty Clause** (§5(a)(1) of the OSH Act).

That was not obvious. The logging standard (1910.266) says it covers "all types of logging,
regardless of the end use of the wood," and OSHA said in 1998 that this reached commercial tree
work. But OSHA's own citation guidance for tree care, **CPL 02-01-045**, draws the line by *scale*,
not end use:

> "a simple tree removal using a chain saw to cut down a tree, and a chipper to dispose of the
> branches and trunk pieces would not likely fall under the Logging operations standard."
>
> "the removal of a tree, or even several trees, from a residential lot, does not constitute
> 'logging' in ordinary language or under the standard."

That is a description of a Sixfold job. Tree *trimming* — limbs and branches, trunk left standing —
is excluded outright. Tree *removal* only becomes "logging" when it looks like logging: a large
tract, many trees, days to months, feller-bunchers and skidders. And before OSHA can cite a
tree-care business under 1910.266 at all, the inspector has to get National Office sign-off.

**What this means in practice:** the rules that apply are the general ones — PPE, first aid, noise,
tools, machine guarding, fuel, electrical, injury reporting — read through the lens of what a
chainsaw and chipper crew actually does. That is what this document is.

**Two caveats.** CPL 02-01-045 (effective 2008-08-21) is now marked *archived* on OSHA's site and
"may no longer represent OSHA policy" — but it states it remains in effect until a tree care
standard is issued, no such standard exists, and OSHA's current tree care page still says the
hazards "are addressed in specific OSHA standards for recordkeeping and general industry." Second:
**a tree care standard is coming.** RIN 1218-AD04 is on the regulatory agenda with a proposed rule
projected for late 2026. When it lands, this document gets revised.

**Colorado is federal-OSHA territory.** Colorado has no state OSHA plan for private employers, so
federal OSHA enforces directly. The office to call: **OSHA Englewood Area Office, 7935 East Prentice
Avenue, Suite 209, Englewood CO 80111, (303) 843-4500.**

**El Paso County is Englewood's, confirmed 2026-09-18** — the open question in draft 1 is closed.
Englewood covers southern and central Colorado including El Paso, Douglas, Elbert, Fremont and
Teller; Denver covers the northern counties. **That is the whole Sixfold service area under one
office**, and it is the Englewood number a crew or the office dials — not Denver.

## Part A — Before anyone works: what Sixfold has to have

### A1. Workers' compensation insurance — required, and Sixfold does not have it

This is the single largest legal exposure the company carries, and it is not an OSHA matter — it
is Colorado law.

**The rule, verbatim from the Colorado Division of Workers' Compensation:**

> "If you have one or more employees working for you in Colorado, you must have workers'
> compensation insurance and maintain it at all times. This applies to all employers, regardless
> of whether the employees are part-time, full-time, or family members."

**Who counts as an employee — this is the part that matters for a small crew:**

> "Anyone who gets paid for their services is presumed to be an employee by law."
>
> "Workers are presumed to be employees unless proven otherwise. To prove that someone is an
> independent contractor and not an employee, the worker must: be free from direction and control
> in the performance of the work; **and** have an independent business doing that specific kind of
> work."
>
> "**Paying someone with a 1099 does not make them a contractor.**"

A crew member who shows up when told, works Sixfold's jobs under Sixfold's direction, with
Sixfold's saws, and does not run their own tree business on the side, is an employee under this
test — regardless of how they are paid.

**LLC members and the rejection form.** The Division's page: "corporate officers and members of
LLCs are considered employees of the company. You must either obtain workers' compensation
coverage for yourself or complete a form indicating you wish to reject that coverage." To reject,
you must "own at least 10% of the company" and "be a member of the LLC." **So Brandon can file a
rejection for himself. Nobody else on the crew can**, unless they are a ≥10% LLC member.

**The penalty, from C.R.S. § 8-43-409:** for every day without the required insurance, a fine of
"not more than two hundred fifty dollars for an initial violation," and "not less than two hundred
fifty dollars or more than five hundred dollars for a second and any subsequent violation." Per
day. On top of that, if someone is hurt while uninsured, the employer pays the claim itself — the
Colorado Uninsured Employer Fund pays the worker and then "will seek reimbursement for expenses
from the uninsured employer," and "if the employer was issued a penalty… the employer must pay that
penalty itself."

**How to get it.** Any of the 500+ licensed carriers, or **Pinnacol Assurance, which "is required
to provide workers' compensation coverage for any Colorado employer"** — the insurer of last
resort, so no Colorado employer can be refused. Premium cannot be deducted from wages.

**Once covered, the employer's standing duties** (Division's list): display the **Notice to
Employer of Injury poster (WC 50)**; keep a record of all lost-time injuries; **report every injury
to the carrier within 10 days**; maintain and provide a **designated medical provider list**; file a
return-to-work form when the worker comes back or leaves.

**Why it also blocks trading in the home market:** Colorado Springs' tree service licence
requires $100,000 workers' compensation as a precondition of applying. See A3.

### A2. General liability insurance — not a state law, but required everywhere that matters

Colorado does not require a tree or landscape business to carry general liability by statute. It is
required by:

- **The Colorado Springs tree service licence** — $1,000,000 commercial general liability minimum,
  a precondition of applying (A3).
- **The CDA pesticide applicator licence** — "evidence of liability insurance on forms provided by
  the Commissioner" must be on file at the time of any application (A3).
- **Customers, HOAs and fire districts** — a homeowner asking "are you insured?" is asking about
  this. The standing brand rule bars *claiming* to be insured until it is true.

Sixfold does not carry GL. This and A1 are the two insurance gaps, and they gate the licence in
A3, so they are one conversation with Brandon, not three.

### A3. Licences

**Colorado has no state licence for tree work or landscaping.** Routine tree care, mowing,
trimming, edging, bed work and planting are unregulated at the state level. Licensing is
**municipal**, and it varies by town — so the question is asked per job site, by address.

**Colorado Springs — the home market, and it licenses the core work.** Verified first-party against
the City Clerk's Tree Service License page (City Code Part 3, 1968 Code §5-64C as amended). A
licence is required of anyone *paid* to "cut, train, prune, shape or remove trees which are 15 feet
or higher," including conifers. **The trigger is the tree's height, not the method — trimming and
pruning count, not just removal.** To hold one: $110 annual business licence · **$1,000,000
commercial general liability** · **$100,000 workers' compensation** · a 40-question exam at 80%,
$120, booked through City Forestry (719) 385-5942 · three photographs of structure pruning or
crown cleaning. **The insurance is the wall, not the exam** — cover is a precondition of applying.
Matthew has handed the licence itself to the team; **standing crew rule until it exists: if a tree
in scope turns out to be 15 ft or taller inside Colorado Springs city limits, stop and call in. Do
not trim it.**

**Other municipalities — not checked.** Monument, Palmer Lake, unincorporated El Paso County,
Douglas County (Sedalia), Woodland Park, Castle Rock. Each needs a first-party check of its own code
before Sixfold works there on trees of any size. Add them to this section as they are confirmed.

**Pesticides — a state licence, and it bites the landscaping side.** Colorado's Pesticide
Applicators' Act (Title 35, Article 9) is administered by the Colorado Department of Agriculture. A
**Commercial Applicator** is "any person… who engages in the business of applying pesticides for
hire." All must be licensed; the business licence fee is **$350**. On top of that, the individual
doing or supervising the work needs a **Qualified Supervisor** licence: "successfully complete the
general exam plus exams in all categories applicable" plus category-specific on-the-job experience.
Liability insurance must be on file with the Commissioner.

  - **Herbicide is a pesticide.** Spraying weeds in a bed for a customer is an application for
    hire. **Fertiliser on its own is not a pesticide** under the Act — but a "weed and feed" product
    is, because it contains herbicide.
  - Sixfold's cost sheet asks about "fertiliser, weed barrier, anything else we buy in." **If any of
    that is herbicide, this licence is required before the next application.** If Sixfold does not
    apply pesticides, nothing here applies — but then nobody on the crew sprays anything, and a
    customer asking for it gets a no.

**811 — Colorado One Call.** Already on the walk sheet: stumps need the 811 locate first, and it
takes three business days. Not a licence, but a legal requirement before any digging, and the
grinder digs.

### A4. Business basics — confirm, do not assume

- **Sixfold LLC** is registered with the Colorado Secretary of State — confirm it is in good
  standing and the periodic report is current. Not verified this session.
- **Sales tax:** labour is generally not taxed in Colorado but tangible goods sold to a customer can
  be. If Sixfold ever sells plants, mulch or materials as a line item rather than passing them
  through at cost, that is a Department of Revenue question. Not verified this session; flagged.

## Part B — On the job: what OSHA requires of the crew

Everything in this part comes from OSHA's General Industry standards as applied to tree care by
CPL 02-01-045 and OSHA's current tree care topic page. Citations are for the office. The crew reads
the bold lines.

### B1. On every job, regardless of the work

**PPE — the employer buys it.** 29 CFR 1910.132(h): protective equipment "shall be provided by the
employer at no cost to employees." The only exceptions that matter here: the employer need not pay
for "non-specialty safety-toe protective footwear (including steel-toe shoes or steel-toe boots)…
provided that the employer permits such items to be worn off the job-site," and need not replace
PPE an employee lost or deliberately damaged. **Everything else — helmets, eye protection, hearing
protection, chaps, gloves — Sixfold pays for.** What is required on a given job comes from a hazard
assessment under 1910.132(d); OSHA's tree care page specifically lists head (1910.135), eye
(1910.133), foot (1910.136) and hand (1910.138) protection.

**A trained first-aider and a kit, on site.** 29 CFR 1910.151(b), verbatim: "In the absence of an
infirmary, clinic, or hospital in near proximity to the workplace which is used for the treatment
of all injured employees, a person or persons shall be adequately trained to render first aid.
Adequate first aid supplies shall be readily available." A rural property is not "in near
proximity" to a hospital. **So: at least one person on every crew holds current first-aid training,
and a stocked kit is on the truck.** OSHA's directive adds that an employer relying on outside
responders instead must weigh "the nature of the hazards at the workplace, distance to the nearest
hospital" — a chainsaw crew in the foothills does not clear that bar. The kit contents: OSHA's
non-mandatory appendix references **ANSI Z308.1**; for a saw crew add trauma dressings and a
tourniquet, because the injury that matters is a deep cut.

**Hearing.** Chain saws and chippers "emit high noise levels when in use" (CPL 02-01-045). 29 CFR
1910.95: above **90 dBA** as an 8-hour average, hearing protection is mandatory; above **85 dBA**,
the employer must run a **hearing conservation program** — noise monitoring, protection at no cost,
baseline and annual hearing tests, training. A chainsaw runs well above both. **Crew rule: ear
protection on whenever a saw or the chipper is running. Office rule: the conservation program is a
real obligation, not optional — it needs a noise measurement and an annual hearing test.**

**Bloodborne pathogens.** OSHA's tree care page lists 1910.1030. A chainsaw cut bleeds; whoever
renders first aid is exposed. The standard's exposure-control requirements apply to designated
first-aiders. **Gloves in the kit, and the first-aider knows to use them.**

### B2. Running a chainsaw

**Chaps are required.** CPL 02-01-045: inspectors "shall determine if employees are required to
wear cut-resistant leg protection while operating chain saws pursuant to §1910.132(a)." The
directive allows only one exception — where the employer can show chaps create a *greater* hazard
in the specific circumstances — and it is the employer's burden to prove it. **Chaps on before the
saw starts. No exceptions on a Sixfold job.**

**The saw must have a kickback device.** 29 CFR 1910.242(a) requires it; the directive says a saw
without one is a citable violation. Chain brake and a low-kickback chain — check before use.

**No drop-starting.** The directive is explicit that "not requiring that chains be started on the
ground or where firmly supported (i.e., no drop starting)" is citable under the General Duty Clause,
because ANSI Z133 §6.3 makes the hazard recognised. **Start the saw on the ground or braced, chain
brake on.**

**Helmet, eye, ear, gloves, boots** — see B1. All four on for saw work.

**Felling.** Sixfold's typical fells are under 30 ft on residential lots — general industry, not the
logging standard. But the logging standard's felling rules (1910.266(h)) are OSHA's own statement of
safe felling practice and the General Duty Clause reaches recognised hazards: **plan the fall,
clear the retreat path, two tree-lengths clear of anyone not felling, watch for hung-up trees and
spring poles, and call in anything over a roof, fence, line or drive — the walk sheet already
counts those separately.**

### B3. Running the chipper

**Machine guarding.** 29 CFR 1910 Subpart O applies to the chipper. Infeed guards and the feed
control bar stay on and working. Never reach into the infeed. Feed butt-end first, stand to the
side of the infeed, never climb onto or over the hopper. Chippers are the most lethal machine in
this trade; OSHA's tree care hazard page exists in large part because of them.

**Within 10 feet of a power line — stop.** If the chipper or the work is within 10 ft of an
electric line, the directive says the chipper requirements in **1910.269(r)(2)** apply *and* that
work is restricted to line-clearance qualified employees. **Nobody on Sixfold's crew is a qualified
line-clearance arborist. So: nothing within 10 ft of a line. Call in.** See B5.

### B4. Fuel, oil and fire

**Flammable liquids.** 29 CFR 1910 Subpart H (1910.106) governs storage, handling and transport of
gas and mix in portable containers. **Approved safety cans, closed, out of the sun, not in the cab.**

**An extinguisher where fuel is stored.** The directive: OSHA's portable-extinguisher standard does
not reach extinguishers for outdoor use, *but* 1910.106(d)(7) requires "suitable fire control
devices, such as portable fire extinguishers" where flammable liquids are stored. **A charged
extinguisher on the truck, next to the fuel.** In fire-mitigation work in fire season, this one is
also just sense.

**Refuelling.** Engine off, cooled, away from ignition sources, no smoking — and for a fire
mitigation crew, on bare ground, not in the slash.

### B5. Near power lines

**Non-qualified workers are prohibited.** The directive: "If employees conducting removal operations
are not qualified employees, this activity would be prohibited by 29 CFR 1910, Subpart S."
Sixfold has no line-clearance qualified employees. **Any tree touching, leaning toward, or within
10 ft of a line is not a Sixfold job until the utility has de-energised or cleared it. The walk
sheet already says call in for anything near a line; this is the rule behind it.**

### B6. Ladders, heights, climbing

OSHA's tree care page lists 1910.23 (ladders) and 1910.67 (aerial lifts). Sixfold does neither at
present — everything is felled from the ground and limbed at four feet. **Standing rule: no
climbing, no ladder work on trees, no aerial lift, until there is training, equipment and a rule for
it.** The walk sheet's "over 80 ft — call in first" reflects this.

### B7. Landscaping work — mowing, trimming, edging, beds

No dedicated OSHA standard. The general ones apply: PPE (eye protection for string trimmers and
edgers, hearing for mowers), machine guarding on the mower (discharge chute guard stays on),
1910.242 for powered hand tools, Subpart H for fuel. **Same crew rules as the saw work, minus chaps.**
If pesticides enter the picture, the CDA licence in A3 does too — and with it the label is the law:
apply exactly as the product label says, wearing what the label says.

## Part D — If someone gets hurt

**First minutes.** Stop the saw. First-aider takes over. Deep cut: direct pressure, tourniquet if
it does not stop, call 911. Do not move a fall casualty. Do not drive them yourself if 911 can come
faster.

**OSHA — federal reporting, and the clock is short.** 29 CFR 1904.39, verbatim: "Within **eight (8)
hours** after the death of any employee as a result of a work-related incident, you must report the
fatality to OSHA." And: "Within **twenty-four (24) hours** after the in-patient hospitalization of
one or more employees or an employee's amputation or an employee's loss of an eye… you must
report." By phone to the nearest Area Office (Englewood, (303) 843-4500), to **1-800-321-OSHA
(1-800-321-6742)**, or by electronic submission at osha.gov — those three, and only those three,
are what the rule permits. **This applies to every employer, however small** — 1904.1 exempts
companies with 10 or fewer employees from keeping the OSHA injury log, but says in the same
paragraph that "all employers covered by the OSH Act must report" these four events.

**Two windows the rule puts on what counts as reportable** — added 2026-09-18, they were missing
from draft 1. The clock above says how fast to report; these say *whether* you have to:

- A fatality is reportable **only if the death occurs within thirty (30) days of the incident.**
  29 CFR 1904.39(b)(6).
- A hospitalisation, amputation or eye loss is reportable **only if it occurs within twenty-four
  (24) hours of the incident.** 29 CFR 1904.39(b)(7). Someone admitted three days later is not a
  reportable event under this rule.

**If you are not sure, report it.** These limits exist so the office can answer "do we have to?"
correctly — they are not an instruction to look for a reason not to call. Over-reporting costs a
phone call; under-reporting is a citation.

**Colorado workers' comp — once insured.** Report every injury to the carrier within **10 days**;
give the injured worker the designated provider list; keep the lost-time record. Before Sixfold is
insured, an injury goes to the Uninsured Employer Fund and the fund comes after Sixfold for the
money (A1).

**Record it.** Even with the 300-log exemption, write down what happened, when, who, and what
changed afterwards. It feeds the close-out sheet's "what was different" box and it is the only
defence if anything is questioned later.

## Part E — What Sixfold does not have today, in order

1. **Workers' compensation.** Required by state law for one employee. Not held. Daily fines. Blocks
   the Springs licence. **First.**
2. **General liability.** Required by the Springs licence and the pesticide licence, and by every
   customer who asks. Not held. **Same conversation as 1.**
3. **Colorado Springs tree service licence.** Blocked on 1 and 2. Until held: no work on trees 15 ft+
   inside city limits.
4. **A designated, trained first-aider on each crew, and a real kit.** Cheap. Required now.
5. **A hearing conservation program** — a noise measurement and an annual hearing test. Required now.
6. **A written PPE hazard assessment** (1910.132(d)) — one page saying what is worn for what.
   Required now, and it is the document this guide's crew rules become.
7. **A decision on pesticides**: either the CDA licence, or a standing "we do not spray."
8. **Municipal licence checks** for every town Sixfold works in beyond Colorado Springs.

Items 4–6 cost almost nothing and can be done this week. Items 1–3 are one conversation with
Brandon and an insurance agent, and they unblock the home market.

## Part F — What is coming

**OSHA's tree care standard.** RIN 1218-AD04 on the federal regulatory agenda; the trade press
reports a proposed rule projected for late 2026 and a final rule in 2027, built on ANSI Z133. When
a final rule is published, **Part B of this document is rewritten** — the standard will replace the
patchwork with specific rules for saws, chippers, felling and climbing. Until then, ANSI Z133 is the
consensus standard OSHA already cites through the General Duty Clause, and a copy in the office
would be worth the cost.
