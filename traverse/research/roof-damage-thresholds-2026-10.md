# Roof damage thresholds by material: hail and wind

**Prepared 2026-10-05 for the door-knocking app (roofing filter).** Research only: nothing in the app changed. The app will filter storm-hit houses to those whose roof material is plausibly damaged by the storm's hail size (MRMS MESH, inches) or wind gust (mph). This file gives the thresholds, where each number comes from, how sure to be, and which wind data to build on. Every figure carries its source and date; where sources disagree and none is authoritative the cell is a range or blank, not a pick.

Grades: **A** = primary source (standards bodies, IBHS research, FM Global, NOAA/NWS/SPC, manufacturer spec sheets, peer-reviewed or academic papers). **B** = named trade outlet, trade association, insurer or manufacturer *publication* (not a spec sheet). **C** = weaker (secondary coverage, undated, or a figure seen only in a search summary).

Two different numbers appear throughout and must not be mixed up:

- **Impact / wind rating**: what a *new* product survived in a laboratory test (UL 2218, FM 4473, FM 4470, ASTM D3161, ASTM D7158). A rating is not a damage threshold; IBHS found in 2014 that "none of the products tested, basic or impact rated, passed more than Class 2 impacts without at least one double impact location failing" (grade A, 2014 purchases).
- **Field damage threshold**: the smallest hail or wind at which damage is actually observed on roofs, mostly aged ones. This is the number the app's filter needs.

## 1. Hail: smallest hail size at which damage is reported

The spine of this table is Haag Engineering's 2002 paper (Marshall, Herzog, Morrison, Smith), the source the NWS itself leaned on when it raised the severe-hail criterion to 1 inch. It has two tables: ice-ball launcher tests (11-year-old asphalt and cedar shingles, 8-year-old built-up, new tiles; "damage" = ≥80% of impacts punctured, fractured, bruised, or cracked the product) and field surveys of hundreds of roofs ("threshold" = onset of damage, all ages). The paper is an AMS conference preprint from an insurer-side engineering firm, not a peer-reviewed journal paper; it is the primary test data everyone else recycles, so it is graded **A** with that caveat.

**What hail "severe" means (for labels in the app):** the NWS defines a severe thunderstorm as one producing hail **1 inch or larger** (quarter) and/or wind gusts **58 mph (50 knots) or greater** and/or a tornado (SPC "About the SPC"). The hail criterion rose from ¾ inch to 1 inch nationwide effective **5 January 2010**; the wind criterion did not change (NWS Public Information Statement, 23 Sep 2009). Reference objects, from the NWS spotter guide: 0.75 penny, 0.88 nickel, **1.00 quarter**, 1.25 half dollar, 1.50 ping-pong ball, 1.75 golf ball, 2.00 lime, 2.50 tennis ball, 2.75 baseball.

**The 1-inch line and asphalt shingles.** Haag 2002 concluded "hailstones one inch in diameter begin causing damage to some of the older, thinner roof products" and recommended the 1-inch severe criterion as "still conservative". IBHS's 2025 peer-reviewed paper (Frontiers in Materials, 29 Sep 2025) pushes the other way for *cumulative* exposure: concentrations of 0.7–1.0 in hail produced granule loss "nearly 30%" of a single 2 in stone and, with weathering, left shingles 9.6× more vulnerable to the next large storm. That is a wear argument, not a single-storm damage threshold; it matters for the age rule, not for the filter cut-off.

## 2. Wind: gust at which damage is commonly expected

Product ratings and field thresholds diverge even more for wind than for hail, because the thing that fails on an asphalt roof is the sealant strip, and ratings are only valid for new, fully sealed shingles.

**Ratings (what a new product is tested to, grade A/B):**

- **ASTM D3161** (fan-induced, 2-hour blow): Class A 60 mph, Class D 90 mph, Class F 110 mph. ARMA leaflet (Nov 2005, B) confirms Class F = tested to 110 mph; Professional Roofing / NRCA coverage (B) gives all three.
- **ASTM D7158** (uplift resistance of *sealed* shingles): Class D up to 90 mph basic wind speed, Class G up to 120 mph, Class H up to 150 mph (ARMA 2005, B; adopted into the IBC 2004 supplement). Based on UL 2390 and ASTM D6381.
- Typical current architectural shingle: "ASTM D3161 (Class F Wind Resistance)", "ASTM D7158 (Class H Wind Resistance)", 130-mph wind-resistance limited warranty (Owens Corning TruDefinition Duration data sheet, 2016, grade A as a spec sheet). The ASTM standard pages themselves were blocked (403), so the class speeds rest on ARMA, NRCA and the spec sheet, not on the standard text.
- Dixon et al. (University of Florida, 2013): these standards "are only valid for shingle products that are fully-sealed and the adhesion performance is defined for new products with no regard for changes in the sealant strength that may occur over the life of a roof."

**Field thresholds (3-second gust, mph):**

The Enhanced Fujita scale (Texas Tech, 2006, adopted by NWS; wind speeds are 3-second gusts "estimated at the point of damage", explicitly "not the same wind as in standard surface observations") gives, for **One- and Two-Family Residences (FR12)**, which it defines as having "asphalt shingles, tile, slate, or metal roof covering":

| DOD | Damage | Expected | Lower bound | Upper bound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Threshold of visible damage | **65** | 53 | 80 |
| 2 | Loss of roof covering material (<20%), gutters and/or awning; loss of vinyl or metal siding | **79** | 63 | 97 |
| 3 | Broken glass in doors and windows | 96 | 79 | 114 |
| 4 | Uplift of roof deck and loss of significant roof covering material (>20%); collapse of chimney; garage doors collapse inward | 97 | 81 | 116 |

The EF scale does **not** split FR12 by roof covering, so for tile, slate and residential metal it is the only A-grade field number and it is the same number. Other EF damage indicators that do name a covering:

## 3. How the app should use this

**Principle.** The filter exists to find leads, so each default is the *lowest credible damage threshold* from the tables above, not the rating. A roofing company edits any number. Every default below names the row it came from.

**Age rule (asphalt only; the only material with a sourced age effect).** Haag 2002: older, thinner roofs have the lowest thresholds, and its lab asphalt was 11 years old. IBHS 2025: weathering plus small hail makes shingles about ten times more vulnerable. IBHS At a Glance: new shingles (both IR and non-IR) offer good protection to 1.5 in. Dixon 2013: unsealing is negligible for 0–6 years and significant from 7 years. A defensible, editable rule that stays inside those sources:

- Roof age (from year built / reroof / remodel) **under 6 years**: raise the asphalt hail default to **1.50** (IBHS new-product finding). Wind: no change; Dixon found almost no unsealing before year 6, but there is no sourced mph.
- **6 to 10 years**: table defaults (1.00 / 1.25).
- **Over 10 years**: use **1.00** for both asphalt types (Haag's 11-year-old laminated panels were damaged by 1.5 in in the lab, and its field threshold for 3-tab "all ages" is 1.00; the laminated 1.25 field figure is for all ages). Wind: show an "aged roof, sealant likely partly unsealed (UF 2013)" flag; no numeric change, because no source gives one.
- Unknown age: table defaults.

**MESH is not a hailstone.** The filter compares a radar *estimate* with thresholds that were measured against *actual* stones. Two cautions, both from A sources: Murillo and Homeyer (J. Appl. Meteor. Climatol., 2019) found NEXRAD MESH needed refitting to align radar size estimates with reported sizes; Wendt and Jirak (Wea. Forecasting, 2021, SPC) note reports are rounded to objects and missing where few people live and MESH is uneven. Our own 5 Aug 2023 read (ROOFING-DATA-CHECK-2026-10.md) had MESH 0.6–1.1 in at points where people reported 1.5–2 in. Practical consequence: do not treat "MESH 0.95 in" as "no 1-inch hail". Suggested app behaviour, which is my design choice and not a sourced figure: show two bands, **"likely"** (MESH at or above the material threshold) and **"possible"** (MESH within 0.25 in below it), and let the company pick which band the crew walks. Keep the word "estimate" on the card.

**Rating badges are not thresholds.** If the app ever shows "Class 4" or "Class H" for a product, it is saying what the product passed when new, not what hail or wind will damage it (IBHS 2014: no tested product, IR or not, cleanly passed above Class 2 in their batch; UF 2013: wind ratings assume full sealing).

**Severe definitions to print verbatim** (NWS/SPC): "A severe thunderstorm produces hail 1 inch in diameter or larger, and/or wind gusts of 58 mph (50 knots) or greater, and/or a tornado." The 1-inch hail criterion has applied nationwide since 5 January 2010; before that it was ¾ inch. For data before 2010, a "severe" label in an archive means ¾ inch.

**Recommendation for "max wind gust near this house per storm day".** Be honest in the UI that no source measures a gust at a house. The most defensible primary approach is:

1. **Primary: the NWS severe thunderstorm warning polygon that covered the house, with its maximum `WINDTAG` over the warning's life** (IEM `watchwarn.py`, `addsvs=yes`, max over SVR+SVS). It exists for every warned storm in Colorado since November 2010, it is an official NWS statement about that place and hour, and it is the number a homeowner will have seen on their phone. Label it "NWS warned up to X mph".
2. **Confirm with reports within a radius** (LSR via IEM for recency; NCEI Storm Events for history, with `MAGNITUDE_TYPE` so the card can say "measured 64 mph 6 km away" or "estimated"). A measured gust within ~5 km should outrank the tag; an estimated one should be shown but not trusted above the tag.
3. **Tie-break and fill-in with the URMA `GUST` daily maximum at the cell** (2.5 km, from 2019, public domain). Show it as "analysis" and expect it to read low in thunderstorms.
4. Nearest ASOS/AWOS gust only as a sanity check; HRRR not at all for history (it is the same information as URMA with more error).

For the filter: a storm day qualifies for a house if (tag ≥ threshold) **or** (a measured report ≥ threshold within ~5 km) **or** (URMA daily max ≥ threshold); the card states which one fired. Thresholds compare against 3-second gusts in principle; tags and reports are gust values, URMA `GUST` is the analysis gust field, and ASOS `peak_wind_gust` is a gust, so no conversion is needed, but the EF-scale numbers are *damage-site* gusts and will read higher than a station 10 km away in the same storm.

### Corrected while researching

- Smith 2013 prints "½ inch" for medium wood shakes. Haag's field figure is 1.50 in and Smith's other rows track Haag exactly, so the table above uses 1.50 and treats "½" as a dropped "1". Not confirmed with the author.
- The search summary for SPC CSVs gave the pattern `YYMMDD_wind.csv`; the file that actually exists is `YYMMDD_rpts_wind.csv` (tested). Both patterns are listed on the page; only the `_rpts_` form was verified.
- The Canadian EF-scale page gives FR12 speeds in km/h only (105 / 125 / 155 / 155); the mph values in §2 come from the original Texas Tech document, and the two agree after conversion.
- NCEI Storm Events wind magnitude is in **knots**, not mph; SPC CSVs and IEM LSR `MAG` for wind are in mph. Mixing them would shift every threshold by 15%.
