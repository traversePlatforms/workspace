Storm Restoration Campaign

Which platforms, who has to own them, and what may not be said on them

Prepared 2026-08-18 Companion to the client profile Scope Named accounts, not channel strategy

Plan. Nothing published, posted, scheduled, registered or applied for.

Restricted — internal, context only

This sheet names the client and repeats their published statistics. Client identity is Tier 4: sector only, at any resolution, and the reason is consent. Nothing here goes into a draft, a caption, alt text, a filename or a payload. An external version has to be written unbranded from scratch, not redacted from this one.

Surge cannot own the platforms this campaign needs.

Not a preference and not a caution. Google's eligibility rules name lead-generation companies as ineligible for a Business Profile, and Local Services Ads require one — so the two highest-intent platforms in this trade are closed to Surge permanently, in this vertical. Meanwhile Colorado's roofing statute governs what any of these accounts may say, and the client's three best-performing claims are the three the statute is about. None of that closes the campaign. It settles who the account holder is, and it turns one question into the gate on everything else.

01

Gate 1 — whose brand runs the ad

Answer this before opening a single account

Three possible answers. Two of them are wrong, and the wrong ones are the convenient ones.

(a) Surge's brand, selling B2B

Right, wrong target

"We build and run field sales teams"

Legitimate, needs nobody's permission, and it uses the accounts Surge already has. It does not produce storm-restoration leads. This is the recruiting and business-development channel — a different sheet.

(b) Surge's brand, selling inspections to homeowners

Never

Produces the right leads and originates every piece of the risk

Under this option Surge advertises restoration work it does not perform, with no roofing registration of its own, no contract with the homeowner, and no shelter under the statute's one carve-out. It also converts the client's insurance-recovery claims into Surge's claims the moment they appear in a Surge ad.

It is the option that looks cheapest and the only one that creates original liability.

(c) The client's brand, Surge operating the accounts

Recommended

Licence, insurance and homeowner contract sit with the company doing the work

Surge runs the accounts, receives the leads, and books the inspections it already performs. This is the only version that survives contact with both Google's rules and Colorado's.

What it requires, in writing

  • Name and logo use, scoped — which platforms, which markets.
  • Which claims Surge may repeat, named individually. Not blanket permission.
  • Who owns each account, and what happens to it if the relationship ends.
  • Roofing registration or licence per state, and a current certificate of insurance on file.
  • Express owner consent for third-party profile management. Google requires it explicitly and suspends profiles claimed without it.

Do not assume (c)

The client may decline, and it is their call. If they do, the honest answer is that this campaign runs on their accounts with their staff, and Surge's digital contribution is the field engine behind it — not a set of accounts Surge controls.

02

Gate 2 — the sentences that cannot be advertised in Colorado

C.R.S. § 6-22-105

C.R.S. § 6-22-105 — Waiver of insurance deductible prohibited

A roofing contractor that performs roofing work, the payment for which will be made from the proceeds of a property and casualty insurance policy … shall not advertise or promise to pay, waive, or rebate all or part of any insurance deductible applicable to the claim for payment for roofing work on the covered residential property.

The consequences are specific, and they land on the campaign rather than in a compliance file:

  • The insurer is not obligated to consider the contractor's estimate of costs. One ad can devalue the estimate on every job that ad produced.
  • The homeowner or the insurer may sue the contractor for damages caused by the violation.

Subsection (3) is the half that reaches a rep at a door

A roofing contractor soliciting in Colorado "shall not claim to be or act as a public insurance adjuster adjusting claims for losses or damages." The statute then carves out one exception: nothing precludes a roofing contractor from discussing, on behalf of the property owner, the scope of repairs with the insurer — when the contractor has a valid contract with the property owner.

Read the timing in that carve-out

The shelter exists after a signed contract. A rep on a doorstep, pre-contract, is outside it. So claim-handling language in an ad, on a landing page, or in a door script is exposed in a way the same language on the client's post-contract customer portal is not.

Held against the client's own published copy

Their site carries all three of the strings this gate exists to stop:

Their claimWhere it appearsVerdict
"$0 Out-of-Pocket for Most Claims" hail page hero stat Never in a Colorado ad. The most dangerous string in their copy.
"97% Insurance Approval Rate" hail page, hail FAQ Their marketing figure, not audited. Repeating it makes it the advertiser's promise.
"we meet with your insurance adjuster… advocate for full coverage" process page Fine for them post-contract. Not usable pre-contract in Surge-run acquisition copy.
"at least 30% more back from your insurance" rep only — nowhere on their site Unusable. Full site bundle searched; no 30% claim of any kind exists.

The 30% figure is the same failure mode as air/aeration, arriving a third time: a load-bearing number that exists only in one person's recollection. It stays out until it appears in the client's own material — and even then, a promised percentage recovery from an insurer needs their written sign-off.

03

Gate 3 — the consent trail

Digital changes the call room's legal position

A digital lead form is a consent record. The moment forms feed the call room, there is a written artefact showing who agreed to be contacted by whom.

  • Colorado requires telemarketer registration. State law requires telemarketers to register and pay an annual fee to the PUC's designated agent, Data Protection Group LLC, with the fee set by the company's total employee count, not just the callers. Whether Surge is registered, and under whose name, is unknown — a question to answer, not an assumption to make.
  • The no-call list is not a one-time download. It updates quarterly and has to be refreshed on a schedule.
  • TCPA got looser, not irrelevant. The FCC's one-to-one consent rule — which would have barred a single form from consenting to contact by more than one company — was vacated by the Eleventh Circuit on 24 January 2025, and repealed by the FCC later that year. A single form naming both companies is workable again. Prior express written consent still governs autodialled and prerecorded telemarketing.
  • The form must name the party that will call. Under gate 1(c) the ad carries the client's brand and the call comes from Surge's room. If Surge is not named on the form, the consent does not cover the call that follows.

Not a reason to skip lead forms

A reason to draft the consent line before the first ad rather than after the first complaint. It is one paragraph, and it unblocks three platforms.

Tier 1 — available now

Uses accounts that already exist

Gate

The brand decision, and nothing else. No licence, no insurance, no screening.

Surge's Google Business Profile — B2B only

Keep it B2B

Not a restoration listing, and it cannot become one

Google's eligibility guidelines name "lead generation agents or companies" as ineligible for a Business Profile, and separately exclude "an ongoing service, class, or meeting at a location that you don't own or have the authority to represent." A storm damage repair — Denver profile run by Surge is both of those at once, and it would be suspended.

A profile for Surge selling outsourced sales teams to companies is a different thing entirely, and it is fine: real business, real address, in-person contact with real clients at Englewood.

If a local restoration listing is wanted, it is the client's listing. They already have a Denver office. Surge asks for manager access under gate 1(c) — the sanctioned route, and the only one that does not risk the profile.

Watch the service-area framing

Service areas on Surge's own profile describe where Surge works, not a limit on where it sells. Englewood runs every campaign including out-of-state ones. Copy has implied Colorado-only before and it was false.

The existing Facebook page

Already live

Connected and in Surge's name

  • Access sits under a personal account. Fine operationally, a single point of failure administratively. Confirm admin roles before the page matters to revenue.
  • It is a Surge-brand asset, so it is a gate 1(a) surface. Restoration content here is client work in Surge's voice — the thing consent does not cover.

Nextdoor and local Facebook groups

Claim now, pays later

The closest digital analogue to a hail swath

A storm creates a same-day neighbourhood conversation — what was that, is my roof fine, who do I call, is this company real. That conversation is geographically identical to the canvass.

  • Nextdoor Business Pages are free to claim, and the free tier includes collecting recommendations and posting to selected neighbourhoods with an adjustable radius.
  • Recommendations are the mechanism, and they expose gate 1 again. A recommendation surfaces in the recommender's own neighbourhood — exactly the geography wanted. But a homeowner recommends the company that fixed their roof, and that is the client. A Surge page cannot collect the recommendations the work earns.
  • It compounds with the canvass rather than adding to it. If a rep irritates a street, that street's Nextdoor is where it gets posted with a company name attached, to precisely the neighbourhood being worked.

Register is everything here

Storm-chasing contractors are the most-flagged category on these surfaces, and being flagged in a neighbourhood is worse than being absent from it. Information-first: what the storm did, what a claim deadline looks like, here is a free inspection. Not an offer post. Drill the door script before the page is live.

Instagram and YouTube

Shoot now, publish later

Both connected; currently brand and recruiting surfaces, correctly

The inspection is the content nobody else in this trade is filming. Same-day, at the door, free. Shoot it from now and bank it; publishing is a later decision.

Clearance is not optional

The photo register governs every frame, and unlisted means not cleared. A storm-damage walkthrough contains the customer's house — an address, a street sign, a house number or a recognisable façade identifies a client's customer. Zoom before clearing, not after.

No LinkedIn company page, no X and no TikTok are connected. Worth knowing; none of them is the gap that matters here.

Tier 2 — gated on the client's credentials

Highest intent in the market, and none of it is Surge's to hold

Gate

Roofing licence, general liability insurance, background checks, and written authorisation. All of it the client's.

Google Local Services Ads

Client's account only

Above search ads, on exactly the queries that matter

Also the clearest illustration of the whole sheet:

  • "Local Services advertisers must have a public and verified Google Business profile." Lead-generation companies are ineligible for a Business Profile. The two rules close the loop — LSA is structurally unavailable to Surge in this trade, permanently.
  • Screening covers licence at state level, general liability insurance, and background checks on the business, the owner, or service professionals depending on category.
  • "On average, this process takes 3–4 weeks to complete after submitting your documents." A four-week screening does not fit inside a hail response — start it out of season or not at all.

Google Search ads on storm and hail intent

Landing page must be the licensed party's

Workable, and every headline runs through gate 2

Expect the compliant version to cost more per lead than any agency benchmark suggests, because the highest-converting phrases in this trade are the prohibited ones.

Meta lead ads into the call room

Best structural fit

Form to same-day inspection booking — short, local, high intent

This feeds the two channels that already exist rather than replacing them. Gated on gate 3: consent line drafted first, both companies named, Surge identified as the caller. Start capped and small. It is a test, not a channel, until a cost per booked inspection exists.

Tier 3 — phase 2

Real, and none of it is the next thing to do

Storm-triggered geo off NOAA data Free, primary

The trigger-event equivalent of a risk-score notice. NOAA's Storm Prediction Center publishes preliminary storm reports daily, with hail and wind archived to 1955, and NCEI's Storm Events Database holds the historical record. Public-domain federal data: a hail swath by date and county, no vendor, no subscription. Pair it with the canvass map and the ad geography is the same shape as the damage.

Spanish-language campaigns Cheap edge, real work

The client's site is fully localised already and Denver metro has the audience. Needs a Spanish-speaking rep on the call, or it generates leads nobody can close.

Inspection walkthroughs on YouTube Search-durable

Unlike feed content, it keeps working. Depends on banked footage and clearance.

Retargeting Phase 3

Only once there is enough traffic to retarget and a landing page worth returning to.

Deliberately not doing

Decisions, so they don't get re-argued

Lead marketplaces

Inverts the business

Angi, Thumbtack, Networx, HomeAdvisor and equivalents

Surge is the lead-generation layer. Buying shared leads at retail to resell into a commission split runs the business backwards. Their model sells the same homeowner to several contractors at once, which turns a same-day free inspection — the actual advantage — into a race.

Any copy carrying the gate 2 strings

Statute, not taste

"$0 out-of-pocket," "we waive your deductible," "we handle your claim," "we negotiate with your insurance," "X% more back."

Bought storm lists and skip-traced call files

No consent trail

They feed the call room and arrive with nothing to show for it — straight into gate 3 with no written consent behind a single dial.

Naming the client in Surge's feed

Consent

Tier 4, and the reason is consent rather than caution. Sector only, at any resolution.

Cold paid social, and TikTok

Wrong shape

Storm restoration is trigger-driven and geographic; there is nothing to convert against until the landing page and the credentials sit with the licensed party. Damage content performs on TikTok and the audience is wrong — it cannot be fenced to a hail swath, and the engine can only service leads a rep can drive to.

Order of operations

Cheap questions first, because they gate expensive work

  1. Decide gate 1. Nothing below this line survives a different answer.
  2. Get the client's answer in writing — name use, which claims may be repeated individually, account ownership, licence and certificate of insurance on file.
  3. Confirm whether Surge is registered as a telemarketer in Colorado, and under whose name.
  4. Legal read on § 6-22-105 against whatever wording survives gate 2 — before the first ad, not after the first complaint.
  5. Draft the consent line for any form that will feed the call room. One paragraph; it unblocks three platforms.
  6. Surge's own profile and page hygiene — B2B framing, admin roles confirmed. Do not create a restoration listing.
  7. Request manager access to the client's Denver Business Profile, and start their LSA screening if it is wanted this season — four weeks minimum.
  8. Claim the neighbourhood surfaces under whichever brand gate 1 produced, and read group rules before posting once.
  9. One capped Meta lead-ad test into same-day inspection booking. Measure cost per booked inspection, not cost per lead.
  10. Bank inspection footage from now, clearance-checked. Publishing is a later decision.

The thing worth advertising, which nobody is advertising

Confirmed 2026-08-18

Same-day inspection Standard

Offered as standard, not as an exception or an upgrade.

Inspected at the door On the spot

A door-to-door rep can inspect during the same visit rather than booking a return trip.

Free Both cases

To the property owner, whether the lead came from a door, a call or a form.

Why these three and not the client's stats

They are service claims about an inspection, not claims about an insurance outcome — which is exactly why they are usable where "$0 out-of-pocket" and "97% approval" are not. The competitive copy in this trade is a wall of regulated insurance promises. Surge's real advantage is an appointment that happens today.

Still open before this becomes an ad

Who carries the liability insurance for the inspection, and what a rep may state about damage at the door. Confirmed does not mean consequence-free — "we inspect" is a service claim, and the copy should not answer those two questions by inference.

Fact-check log

What was verified, corrected, and left open

Verified — statute and first-party documentation

  • C.R.S. § 6-22-105, "Waiver of insurance deductible prohibited": (1) prohibits advertising or promising to pay, waive or rebate any part of the deductible where payment comes from insurance proceeds; (2)(a) the insurer need not consider that contractor's estimate; (2)(b) the owner or insurer may sue for damages; (3) prohibits claiming to be or acting as a public insurance adjuster, with a carve-out for discussing scope of repairs with the insurer once a valid contract with the owner exists — C.R.S. § 6-22-105. Read from a statutory reproduction, not the state's own publication — both Justia and Colorado's statute host refused automated fetch. Text is verbatim and self-consistent; a lawyer should read it from the official source before copy ships.
  • Public adjuster licensing at C.R.S. § 10-2-417, referenced by 6-22-105(3) itself.
  • Google Business Profile eligibility: "lead generation agents or companies" are ineligible; so is "an ongoing service, class, or meeting at a location that you don't own or have the authority to represent"; third-party managers must "never claim a Business Profile without express consent from the business owner"; and "to qualify for a Business Profile, a business must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours" — Business eligibility and ownership guidelines.
  • Local Services Ads: "Local Services advertisers must have a public and verified Google Business profile to advertise with Local Service Ads." Screening covers business and owner licence at state level, general liability insurance, and background checks varying by category. "On average, this process takes 3–4 weeks to complete after submitting your documents." — Business screening and verification requirements, US.
  • Colorado telemarketer registration: state law requires telemarketers to register and pay an annual fee to the PUC's designated agent, Data Protection Group LLC, with the fee based on the company's total employee count rather than callers alone — Colorado PUC, No-Call List Telemarketer Information.
  • TCPA one-to-one consent rule vacated by the Eleventh Circuit on 24 January 2025, Insurance Marketing Coalition Ltd. v. FCC, No. 24-10277, for exceeding the FCC's statutory authority; the FCC subsequently repealed it — 11th Cir. opinion.
  • NOAA storm data is free and public-domain: SPC publishes preliminary storm reports and archived hail and convective wind reports from 1955; NCEI's Storm Events Database holds records from 1950 — SPC storm reports and NCEI Storm Events Database.
  • Nextdoor Business Pages are free to claim; the free tier includes collecting recommendations and posting to selected neighbourhoods with an adjustable radius — Nextdoor Business. Carried forward from the Sixfold platform sheet's verified log, same source.
  • Surge's connected accounts, read 2026-08-18: Facebook page Surge Inc (held via a personal account), YouTube Surge Inc. (Surge FTW), Instagram @surge.ftw, plus a channel-less personal YouTube connection. No LinkedIn company page, no X, no TikTok.

Corrected during research

  • Every first-page result for LSA roofing requirements was an SEO agency blog, and they contradicted each other. One stated a $1M general liability floor, another "between $100,000 and $1,000,000." Google's own documentation states no figure at all. Neither number is used. The same sources disagreed on whether the badge is "Google Guaranteed" or "Google Verified" — also not used.
  • The telemarketer fee table on the Colorado PUC's own page is labelled 2017. First-party and stale at the same time. The registration requirement is used; the figures are not. Get the current schedule from the designated agent.

Unresolved — flagged, not guessed

  • Whether the client will authorise name use and third-party account management at all. Gate 1(c) assumes yes, and the whole sheet forks on it.
  • Their roofing registration or licence per state, and which legal entity holds it.
  • Whether Surge is registered as a Colorado telemarketer, and under whose name.
  • Who insures a Surge rep performing a free inspection, and what a rep may state about damage pre-contract.
  • Whether deductible language is separately restricted for a non-contractor advertiser promoting a contractor's services. 6-22-105 binds "a roofing contractor"; whether it reaches Surge as agent is a lawyer's question, and the practical answer is not to test it.
  • The client's existing referral programme — their site carries per-user referral links and configurable referral fee payouts, currently marked inactive. A digital lead mechanism may already exist, and building a second one before asking would be wasted work.
  • The "30% more back" claim — rep-sourced, absent from their entire site. Unusable, and it should not be repeated internally either until sourced.
  • 20 states versus "40+ states" — the rep and the site disagree and neither is authoritative. Surge's copy states neither, so it matters only if someone asks for a number.
