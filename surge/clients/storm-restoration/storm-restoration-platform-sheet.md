## The finding that reorders everything

**Surge cannot own the platforms this campaign needs.** Not a preference and not a caution — Google's own eligibility rules exclude it, and Colorado's roofing statute governs what the account may say once it exists.

The Sixfold sheet opened on a twenty-dollar domain blocking a free channel. This one opens on something harder: the licence, the insurance, the contract with the homeowner and the right to advertise all sit with VI-A, and Surge's entire contribution — leads, calls, inspections — is the one activity Google names as ineligible for the anchor platform in this trade.

That does not close the campaign. It changes who the account holder is, and it makes one question the gate on everything else.

## Gate 1 — whose brand runs the ad

Answer this before opening a single account. Every other decision in this sheet forks on it, and two of the three answers are wrong.

### (a) Surge's own brand, selling B2B

"We build and run field sales teams." Legitimate, needs nobody's permission, uses the accounts Surge already has. **It does not produce storm-restoration leads.** This is the recruiting and business-development channel, and it is a different sheet.

### (b) Surge's own brand, selling storm inspections to homeowners

Produces exactly the right leads and carries every piece of the risk. Under this option Surge is the advertiser for restoration work it does not perform, with no roofing registration of its own, no contract with the homeowner, and no shelter under the one carve-out in the statute (see gate 2). It also makes VI-A's insurance-recovery claims into Surge's claims the moment they appear in a Surge ad.

**Do not do this.** It is the option that looks cheapest and is the only one that creates original liability.

### (c) VI-A's brand, Surge operating the accounts as its agent

The licence, the insurance and the homeowner contract sit with the company that does the work. Surge runs the accounts, receives the leads, and books the inspections it already performs. This is the version that survives contact with both Google's rules and Colorado's.

**What it requires, in writing:**

- Name and logo use, scoped — which platforms, which markets.
- **Which claims Surge may repeat**, named individually. Not blanket permission.
- Who owns each account, and what happens to it if the relationship ends.
- Roofing registration or licence per state, and a current certificate of insurance on file.
- Express owner consent for third-party profile management — Google requires it explicitly and suspends profiles claimed without it.

**Recommendation: (c) for anything homeowner-facing, (a) for Surge's own feed, never (b).** And note that (c) is not a formality Surge can assume — VI-A may decline, in which case the honest answer is that this campaign runs on their accounts with their staff, and Surge's digital contribution is the field engine behind it.

## Gate 2 — the sentences that cannot be advertised in Colorado

**C.R.S. § 6-22-105, "Waiver of insurance deductible prohibited."** A roofing contractor whose payment will come from property and casualty insurance proceeds **shall not advertise or promise to pay, waive, or rebate all or part of any insurance deductible** applicable to the claim.

The consequences are specific, and they land on the campaign rather than on a compliance file:

- The insurer **is not obligated to consider the contractor's estimate** of costs. One ad can devalue the estimate on every job it produced.
- The homeowner **or the insurer** may sue the contractor for damages caused by the violation.

**Subsection (3) is the second half, and it is the one that touches a rep at a door.** A roofing contractor soliciting in Colorado "shall not claim to be or act as a public insurance adjuster adjusting claims for losses or damages." The statute then carves out one exception: nothing "precludes a roofing contractor from discussing, on behalf of the property owner, the scope of repairs with a property and casualty insurer **when the roofing contractor has a valid contract with the property owner**."

Read the timing in that carve-out. The shelter exists *after* a signed contract. A Surge rep on a doorstep, pre-contract, is outside it — so claim-handling language in an ad, a landing page or a door script is exposed in a way the same language on VI-A's post-contract customer portal is not.

### Held against the client's own published copy

VI-A's site carries all three of the strings this gate exists to stop:

| Their claim | Where it appears | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **"$0 Out-of-Pocket for Most Claims"** | hail page hero stat | **Never in a Colorado ad.** The single most dangerous string in their copy. |
| **"97% Insurance Approval Rate"** | hail page, hail FAQ | Their marketing figure, not audited. Repeating it makes it the advertiser's promise. |
| **"we meet with your insurance adjuster… advocate for full coverage"** | process page | Fine for them post-contract. Not usable pre-contract in Surge-run acquisition copy. |
| **"VI-A promises at least 30% more back from your insurance"** | rep only — **nowhere on their site** | **Unusable.** Searched the full site bundle; no 30% claim of any kind exists. |

The 30% figure is the [[detail-hardens-through-repetition]] pattern arriving a third time: a load-bearing number that exists only in one person's recollection. It stays out until it turns up in VI-A's own material, and even then a promised percentage recovery from an insurer needs their written sign-off.

## Gate 3 — the consent trail, because digital changes the call room

This is the gate nobody asks about, and it is the one that alters an operation already running. **A digital lead form is a consent record.** The moment forms feed the call room, the calling operation's legal position changes, because there is now a written artefact showing who consented to what.

- **Colorado requires telemarketer registration.** State law requires telemarketers to register and pay an annual fee to the PUC's designated agent, Data Protection Group LLC, with the fee set by the total number of employees at the company — not just the callers. **Whether Surge is registered, and under whose name, is unknown.** That is a question to answer, not an assumption to make.
- **The no-call list is not a one-time download.** It updates quarterly and has to be refreshed on a schedule.
- **TCPA got looser, not irrelevant.** The FCC's one-to-one consent rule — which would have barred a single form from consenting to contact by more than one company — was **vacated by the Eleventh Circuit on 24 January 2025** in *Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC*, and the FCC repealed it in 2025. So a single form naming both companies is workable again. Prior express written consent still governs autodialled and prerecorded telemarketing.
- **The form must name the party that will call.** Under gate 1(c) the ad carries VI-A's brand and the call comes from Surge's room. If Surge is not named on the form, the consent does not cover the call that follows.

None of this is a reason to skip lead forms. It is a reason to draft the consent line before the first ad, not after the first complaint.

## Tier 1 — available now

**Gate:** the brand decision, and nothing else. These use accounts that already exist.

### Surge's own Google Business Profile — B2B only

**Not** a storm-restoration listing. Google's eligibility guidelines name **"Lead generation agents or companies"** as ineligible for a Business Profile, and separately exclude "an ongoing service, class, or meeting at a location that you don't own or have the authority to represent." A "storm damage repair — Denver" profile run by Surge is both of those at once, and it would be suspended.

A profile for Surge selling outsourced sales teams to companies is a different thing and is fine: real business, real address, in-person contact with real clients at Englewood.

**If a local restoration listing is wanted, it is VI-A's listing.** They already have a Denver office. Surge asks for manager access under gate 1(c) — which is the sanctioned route, and the only one that does not risk the profile.

> **Watch the service-area framing.** If Surge's own profile lists service areas, they describe where Surge works, not a limit on where it sells. Englewood runs every campaign including out-of-state ones, and copy has implied Colorado-only before and been wrong.

### The existing Facebook page

`Surge Inc` exists and is connected. Two things worth knowing before it carries a campaign:

- **Access sits under a personal account** (Myles Zoutte holds the page). Fine operationally, a single point of failure administratively. Confirm admin roles before the page matters to revenue.
- It is a Surge-brand asset, so it is a gate 1(a) surface. Restoration content here is client work in Surge's voice, which is the thing consent does not cover.

### Neighbourhood surfaces after a storm — Nextdoor and local Facebook groups

**This is the closest digital analogue to a hail swath, and the register is everything.** A storm creates a same-day neighbourhood conversation: what was that, is my roof fine, who do I call, is this company real. That conversation is geographically identical to the canvass.

- **Nextdoor Business Pages are free to claim**, and the free tier includes collecting recommendations and posting to selected neighbourhoods with an adjustable radius.
- **Recommendations are the mechanism, and they expose gate 1 again.** A recommendation surfaces in the recommender's own neighbourhood — which is exactly the geography wanted. But the homeowner recommends *the company that fixed their roof*, and that is VI-A. A Surge page cannot collect the recommendations the work earns.
- **Storm-chasing contractors are the most-flagged category on these surfaces**, and being flagged in a neighbourhood is worse than being absent from it. The register is the information-first one: what the storm did, what a claim deadline looks like, here is a free inspection. Not an offer post.
- **It compounds with the canvass rather than adding to it.** If a rep irritates a street, that street's Nextdoor is where it gets posted with a company name attached, to precisely the neighbourhood being worked. Drill the door script before the page is live.

### Instagram and YouTube — the assets already exist

`@surge.ftw` and `Surge Inc. (Surge FTW)` are both connected. Currently brand and recruiting surfaces, correctly.

**The inspection is the content nobody else in this trade is filming.** Same-day, at the door, free — see the closing section. Shoot it from now and bank it; publishing is a later decision.

Clearance is not optional: [photo-clearance-register](../../brand/photos/photos-notes.md) governs every frame, unlisted means not cleared, and a storm-damage walkthrough carries **the customer's house** in it. An address, a street sign, a house number or a recognisable façade identifies a client's customer. Zoom before clearing, not after.

**No LinkedIn company page, no X and no TikTok are connected.** Worth knowing; none of them is the gap that matters here.

## Tier 2 — gated on the client's licence, insurance and written authorisation

The highest-intent platforms in this trade. Every one of them verifies credentials Surge does not hold, which means every one of them is a VI-A account.

### Google Local Services Ads

Top of the page, above search ads, on exactly the queries that matter. Also the clearest illustration of the whole sheet:

- **"Local Services advertisers must have a public and verified Google Business profile."** Lead-generation companies are ineligible for a Business Profile. The two rules close the loop: **LSA is structurally unavailable to Surge**, in this trade, permanently.
- Screening covers licence at state or province level, general liability insurance, and background checks on the business, the owner, or service professionals depending on category.
- **"On average, this process takes 3–4 weeks to complete after submitting your documents."** Sequence it early or it becomes the thing everything waits on.

So: VI-A's LSA account, VI-A's credentials, Surge granted access. Started now if it is wanted for the next storm season, because a four-week screening does not fit inside a hail response.

### Google Search ads on storm and hail intent

Workable, and the landing page must belong to the licensed contractor. Gate 2 governs every headline. Note that the highest-converting phrases in this trade are the prohibited ones, so expect the compliant version to cost more per lead than an agency benchmark suggests.

### Meta lead ads into the call room

**The best structural fit for the engine Surge already runs.** Form to same-day inspection booking is a short, local, high-intent path, and it feeds the two channels that already exist rather than replacing them. Gated on gate 3 — consent line drafted first, both companies named, Surge identified as the caller. Start capped and small; this is a test, not a channel, until a cost per booked inspection exists.

## Tier 3 — phase 2

- **Storm-triggered geo campaigns off NOAA data.** The trigger-event equivalent of Sixfold's risk-score notice, and it is free and primary. NOAA's Storm Prediction Center publishes preliminary storm reports daily, with hail and wind reports archived back to 1955, and NCEI's Storm Events Database carries the historical record. Public-domain federal data: a hail swath by date and county, no vendor, no subscription. Pair it with the canvass map and the ad geography is the same shape as the damage.
- **Spanish-language campaigns.** VI-A's site is fully localised already, and Denver metro has the audience. Cheap edge, real work — it needs a Spanish-speaking rep on the call, or it generates leads nobody can close.
- **Inspection walkthrough video on YouTube.** Search-durable, unlike feed content. Depends on banked footage and clearance.
- **Retargeting.** Only once there is enough traffic to retarget and a landing page worth returning to.

## Deliberately not doing

**Lead marketplaces — Angi, Thumbtack, Networx, HomeAdvisor and equivalents.** The structural objection from the Sixfold sheet applies, and a sharper one applies on top of it: **Surge is the lead-generation layer.** Buying shared leads at retail to resell into a commission split inverts the business. Their model sells the same homeowner to several contractors at once, which turns a same-day free inspection — the actual advantage — into a race.

**Any copy carrying the gate 2 strings.** "$0 out-of-pocket," "we waive your deductible," "we handle your claim," "we negotiate with your insurance," "X% more back." Not a voice call, a statute.

**Bought storm lists and skip-traced call files.** They feed the call room and arrive with no consent trail, straight into gate 3 with nothing to show for it.

**Naming VI-A in Surge's own feed.** Tier 4, and the reason is consent, not caution. Sector only.

**Paid social prospecting to cold audiences.** Storm restoration is trigger-driven and geographic. There is nothing to convert against until the landing page and the credentials sit with the licensed party.

**TikTok.** Damage content performs there and the audience is wrong — it cannot be fenced to a hail swath, and the engine can only service leads a rep can drive to.

## The thing worth advertising, which nobody is advertising

Three facts, confirmed 2026-08-18, that were nowhere in Surge's copy until this week:

- **Same-day inspection**, offered as standard.
- **Inspection at the door, on the spot** — a door-to-door rep can inspect during the same visit rather than booking a return trip.
- **Free**, to the property owner, in both cases.

These are the whole digital hook, and the reason is structural: they are **service claims about an inspection, not claims about an insurance outcome.** That is precisely why they are usable where "$0 out-of-pocket" and "97% approval" are not. The competitive copy in this trade is a wall of insurance promises, all of it regulated. Surge's real advantage is an appointment that happens today.

**Still open before this becomes an ad:** who carries the liability insurance for the inspection, and what a rep may state about damage at the door. Confirmed does not mean consequence-free — "we inspect" is a service claim, and the copy should not answer those two questions by inference.

## Fact-check log

**Verified — statute and first-party documentation:**

- **C.R.S. § 6-22-105**, "Waiver of insurance deductible prohibited": subsection (1) prohibits a roofing contractor paid from property and casualty insurance proceeds from advertising or promising to pay, waive or rebate any part of the deductible; (2)(a) the insurer is not obligated to consider that contractor's estimate; (2)(b) the property owner or insurer may sue for damages; (3) prohibits claiming to be or acting as a public insurance adjuster, with a carve-out for discussing scope of repairs with the insurer **once a valid contract with the property owner exists** — [C.R.S. § 6-22-105](https://codes.findlaw.com/co/title-6-consumer-and-commercial-affairs/co-rev-st-sect-6-22-105/). **Read from a statutory reproduction, not the state's own publication** — Justia and Colorado's own statute host both refused automated fetch. The text is verbatim and self-consistent, and a lawyer should read it from the official source before copy ships.
