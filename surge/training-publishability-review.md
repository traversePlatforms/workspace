# Training material — publishability review

**Reviewed:** 2026-08-06. Covers the seven `.docx` files added to [training-source/](training-source/) plus `Law of averages.docx`.

**Why this file exists:** [brand-voice-company.md](brand-voice-company.md) §8 open question 2 — *"Which training material is publishable?"* The content engine in §6 runs on this material, so the question gates everything. This is the answer, per document.

**Bottom line:** the system is strong, coherent, and genuinely teachable. About a third of it is publishable close to as-written. About a third needs reframing. About a third would actively damage the brand if published, because it describes influence technique from the operator's side, and that is the exact thing critics allege.

## The publishable third

**Successful Business Practices** is the strongest asset here and I'd start with it. It's specific, unglamorous, and true of any field job — "if you're not early you're late," "every door, every floor, every store," "work a full 8 hours or until you hit your goal." Step 7 is the best line in the whole set: *"using the systems to perform is important, but using them to learn the business is everything."* That sentence does more brand work than any culture post, and it's publishable verbatim.

**SEE**, **WWW**, and **KISS** are clean pedagogy. They teach a new rep to introduce themselves and explain a cause briefly. Nothing about them is adversarial and they demonstrate the teaching claim in §1 rather than asserting it.

**AIR** and **FFF** are publishable *as frameworks* — acknowledging an objection before responding is ordinary communication skill, taught in every service job. It's the scripted rebuttals that aren't.

**Steps to Rehash**, steps 2 and 3 — showing gratitude and telling a donor what their money did — is genuinely good practice and reads well.

That is enough for six to eight posts without touching anything sensitive.

## Four things I'd flag before any of this goes near a draft

### 1. The Mehrabian statistic is wrong

`Gifts.docx` teaches: *"55% of communication is body language, 38% is the tone of voice, 7% is the actual words spoken."*

This is the most frequently debunked statistic in sales training. It comes from two small 1967 Mehrabian studies about how people infer **liking** when tone and word conflict — single words, spoken by women, in a lab. Mehrabian spent decades objecting to the generalisation, stating it was never meant to apply to communication broadly.

Two consequences. It should not appear in a public post — it is trivially checkable and a comment section will do it in minutes. And separately, it is being taught to reps as fact, which is worth telling whoever owns the curriculum. The underlying point — *"it's not what you say, it's how you say it"* — is sound and survives without the fake numbers.

### 2. "Every day 4 kids get diagnosed in Colorado"

`Steps to Rehash.docx` gives this as a bullet-point example. If reps say this on doorsteps, it is a live factual claim about a charity's cause with no source attached. Under the research standard it can't go in a post, and it's worth confirming it's sourced before it goes in a pitch either. Not flagging it as false — flagging that nothing here shows where it came from.

### 3. The scarcity language has to be literally true

`Gifts.docx` teaches fear-of-loss phrasing: *"Last day," "Sold out," "Miss out," "Too late."* Where those are accurate, fine. Where a campaign isn't actually ending, they're false statements made to induce a payment. That distinction doesn't affect a post — none of this is publishable anyway — but it's the kind of thing that produces the complaints that produce the Reddit threads.

### 4. The disclosure instruction — the one that matters most

`Dos and Don'ts.docx` pairs these:

- **Do:** "Mention that it is month to month"
- **Don't:** "Mention they can cancel any time"

Read alongside the clawback in [brand/notes/hiring.md](brand/notes/hiring.md) — the rep loses money if the donor cancels inside three months — that is an instruction to withhold a material term from someone signing a recurring payment, given by a party with a direct financial interest in them not knowing it.

I'm not a lawyer and this isn't a legal opinion. But charitable solicitation is regulated at state level, recurring-payment disclosure is a live consumer-protection area, and "month to month" is not a substitute for a cancellation right. This is worth putting in front of whoever handles compliance rather than leaving in a training doc.

For my purposes the rule is simpler and absolute: **this line never appears in any public content**, and no draft describes Surge's disclosure practices at all until it's resolved.

## The structural problem with publishing GIFTS and Closing Circle

These two are the best-constructed documents in the set and the least publishable, which is an awkward combination.

**GIFTS** names its own mechanism: greed, indifference, fear of loss, urgency. *"Be the opposite of a salesperson. No one wants to be sold."* — that is instruction to present as not-selling while selling. **Closing circle** loops Object → Rebuttal → Greed → Urgency → Fear of Loss → Close → Rehash, *"at least 3 times."*

There's nothing unusual here by industry standards. The problem is purely one of audience. Techniques for influencing a decision read as craft from the inside and as manipulation from the outside, and the outside is who reads a public post. Publishing GIFTS would hand critics a primary-source document written by Surge, which is a materially worse position than the current one where the allegations are secondhand.

The same applies to the objection rebuttals. *"I must ask my spouse / Wouldn't want to get in the way, let me tell you what you can tell them"* is a workaround for a stated decision-maker, and it reads that way on the page.

**None of this is a judgement about running the training.** It's a judgement about which documents survive contact with a public audience, which is the only question this file is answering.

## Correction — Rehash was over-restricted

**Added 2026-08-06.** This review originally treated the rehash upsell as unpublishable in full. That was wrong, and it produced a bad asset: a five-step graphic with step 5 renamed to dodge the restriction.

The user's objection was the right one — *"it's not really 5 steps if you cut step 5."* Renaming a step so it can survive a restriction doesn't publish four steps honestly, it publishes five steps dishonestly.

**Upselling is not shameful.** Offering a second item after a first purchase is ordinary commerce. What reads badly is the operator-side vocabulary — *"double your profit," "never stop at a yes," "close gap on a no," "Remember Everyone Has Another Sale Hidden"* — plus the secure-payment-before-pitching sequencing. Those are the restricted parts.

Described accurately — thank the person, say what their support pays for, make one further offer, and call it a second ask — **the step publishes fine and keeps the count honest.**

**Generalise this before restricting anything else:** separate the *practice* from the *vocabulary*. §5 of the company spec already states the principle — concealing something defensible is what makes it look indefensible.

## The lineage issue

The vocabulary here — SEE, WWW, KISS, GIFTS, AIR, FFF, ABC, Rehash, impulse, the Jones Effect, "turn and burn," "smother the territory," Law of Averages — is the standard direct-sales training canon, near-verbatim, shared across a large family of organisations descended from the same lineage.

This is not a criticism; §1 already says the method isn't differentiated and Surge says so. Two practical consequences:

1. **It confirms the never-claim-proprietary rule with evidence.** Anyone who has worked in the industry recognises these acronyms immediately. A proprietary claim wouldn't just be unsupportable, it would be recognisably false to the most informed part of the audience.
2. **Publishing the distinctive terms self-identifies the lineage** to exactly the readers already primed to make the devilcorp connection. Teaching *the underlying skill* in Surge's own words carries none of that cost. Prefer "here's how we teach a rep to handle the first no" over "here's our GIFTS framework."

## What's missing, and it's the good stuff

Every document here is the **what**. The [template](training-source/_TEMPLATE.md) asks for four things none of them have, and per §6 those are where the strongest posts live:

- **How it's actually taught** — classroom, roleplay, ride-along, drill? How long? Who runs it?
- **What people get wrong** — the common failure and what it looks like when someone hasn't got it. §6 is explicit that the mistake is more interesting than the technique, and it's the single biggest gap.
- **When it's taught** — day one, week one, month two.
- **Field examples** — things that actually happened.

A post built from "here are the 5 steps" is a listicle anyone could write. A post built from "most reps blow step 2 in the same specific way, and here's what it sounds like" can only come from Surge. The second kind needs material that doesn't exist yet, and it's worth more than everything above.
