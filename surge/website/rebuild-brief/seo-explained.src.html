Making the site findable for what Surge actually does.

SEO is not a trick played on Google. It is making sure that when a company searches for the thing Surge sells, the site can be found, understood and trusted. Most of the work is accuracy and structure. This explains what was done, in plain terms, and what it is reasonable to expect from it.

The starting position

The site live today was built competently — it uses a standard WordPress SEO plugin and the basic tags are all present. The problem is not that the SEO is missing. The problem is what it says:

PageThe line Google shows today
HomeDenver Outsource Sales & Marketing | Business Consulting Firm
Our FocusDenver Sales Team Training, Management, & Consulting Services
TeamDenver Professional Sales Team | Sales Consulting & Growth Company
ClientsDenver Marketing Consulting | Sales Firm | Outsourced Sales Team
ContactDenver Sales Training Team | B2B Sales & Marketing Consulting Firm

Three problems in that one column.

The city is wrong, and it is doing the most work. Five of seven titles lead with Denver. The office is in Englewood, and the company's own settled position is that it has clients in multiple states — the site reading as Denver-only was identified as actively costing out-of-state enquiries.

The category is wrong. “Business consulting firm”, “fractional sales”, “lead gen”, “B2B”. Surge hires, trains and runs field sales teams — door to door, phone, events and booths. A company searching for what Surge does would not use those words, and a company searching for those words would be disappointed by the call.

Every title is too long. All five above run 64 to 70 characters. Google cuts the line at roughly 60, so the end of each one is never seen.

The short version for the meeting

The old site was optimised — accurately — for a company Surge isn't, in a city Surge isn't in. The work was not adding SEO. It was pointing the existing SEO at the real business.

The work

Six things, and what each one is for.

1 · The line in the search result

What it is. Every page has a title and a one-sentence description. That pair is the entire advert Google shows — it is what somebody reads before deciding whether to click.

What was wrong. The careers page description ran 248 characters, so a third of it never appeared. Four more pages were over the limit. The team page had a 23-character description. The ten staff pages shared one sentence with a name swapped in.

What was done. Every one rewritten to fit, and to say something specific. The staff pages now carry a line from that person's own bio rather than a template.

2 · Saying where the company works, correctly

What it is. Search engines infer geography from the words on the page.

What was wrong. Two of the new pages had picked up the same habit as the old site — pairing the service with the city, which frames the whole business as local.

What was done. Both rewritten. Englewood is now named only where the office or hiring is genuinely the subject — the careers page keeps it, because somebody applying needs to know where the job is. Elsewhere the phrase is “clients in multiple states”.

3 · Telling Google what the pages are, not just what words they contain

What it is. A small machine-readable block that states plainly “this is an article”, “this is the company, here is its phone number”, “this page sits inside this section”. Search engines use it to understand a site rather than guess at it.

What was wrong. One page out of 28 had any.

What was done. Now 27 do — every page states where it sits in the site, and the six articles declare themselves as articles with a headline and a publisher.

4 · Six articles that no longer dead-end

What it is. Search engines follow links. A page nothing links to is a page they are slow to find and slow to value.

What was wrong. Each article had exactly one link pointing at it, from the blog index, and none pointed at each other. A reader who finished one had nowhere to go.

What was done. Each article now recommends two others at the foot. Every article gained two inbound links, and no article was left out.

5 · The site works properly on a phone

What it is. Google ranks on the phone version of a site, not the desktop one.

What was done. The whole site is tested across nine real device sizes — three phones, a phone turned sideways, three tablets and two iPads in landscape. It passes all of them. Two real faults were found and fixed: every phone was downloading two copies of the homepage video, and every form field was small enough to make iPhones zoom in and stay zoomed.

6 · A map for the crawler

What it is. A sitemap file listing every page worth indexing, submitted to Google.

What was done. It exists, lists exactly the 26 public pages, and correctly leaves out the two that should never appear in a search result. Submitting it is a launch-day step.

Judgement

What was deliberately not done.

Several things that would show up as “more SEO” were left undone on purpose. Each is a decision worth being able to defend.

Not doneReason
Publication dates on the articles Google prefers a date on an article. Nothing has been published yet, so there is no true date to give, and a date that is not true is worse than a missing one. The dates go in on launch day, and each article carries a note saying so.
Naming clients for the keyword value Client names are good for search and barred by the company's own rule. No client hired Surge in order to become marketing material. Logos are separately cleared and can be used as logos.
Repeating “Denver” to keep the existing local rankings It would hold some rankings in the short term at the cost of being wrong about the business. The company decided its geography is “clients in multiple states”.
Padding the ten staff pages They run 100–180 words each, which is short. They exist to prove real people work here, not to rank. Padding them with filler would weaken the thing they are good at.
Installing Google Analytics The privacy policy states in published copy that the site runs no analytics or advertising trackers. Adding one contradicts a promise the site makes. Search Console is used instead — it is first-party, needs no tracker on the page, and answers the same questions.

What to expect, honestly

Nothing happens on day one. The site has been hidden from search engines throughout the build, deliberately. When that is switched off, Google has to crawl 26 pages it has never seen, work out how they relate, and decide what to show. That takes weeks, not days.

Some existing rankings will move, and some will drop. The site currently ranks for Denver consulting terms. Those terms describe a business Surge is not in, so losing them is the intended outcome — but it is a real change and it is worth saying out loud rather than being surprised by it.

The realistic gain is being findable for the right thing. A company looking to outsource a field sales team, or somebody searching the company by name to check whether it is real, will find an accurate site with ten named people, a working phone number and a form that reaches somebody. The old site fails all four of those.

How it will be measured

Google Search Console — what people searched, what was shown, what was clicked. Free, first-party, no tracking script. Submitted on launch day.

SiteGround's own traffic statistics — server-side visitor numbers, the same kind of figures as the July 2026 baseline already on file, so the before and after are comparable.

The form log — every enquiry through the site is recorded, so enquiries can be counted directly rather than inferred.

One item that is not an SEO job but costs enquiries today

The “Call Us” button on the live homepage dials 719 315 7742, which is not Surge's number, while the same page displays the correct 720 number next to it. Anyone tapping the main button on a phone calls the wrong line. This is fixable in WordPress immediately and does not need to wait for the new site.
