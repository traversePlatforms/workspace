Onboarding · What the morning block is for

Training week is not
the learning week.

Forgetting is not a discipline problem. It is the documented default, and it has been measured for a hundred and forty years. What you do the following week is the part that is actually up for grabs.

01
Set the goal
The number the room is working to, said before anything else.
02
Drill it on the board
Written out in front of everyone, not read off a page.
03
One taught subject
A single thing that day. Not a syllabus, not a refresher.
04
Paired practice
Say it out loud to another rep. Trainers work the new ones.

Murre & Dros · PLOS ONE · 2015

Ebbinghaus’ 1880 forgetting curve replicates. One subject, 70 hours of learning, relearned at six intervals.

The authors add one wrinkle: the curve is “not completely smooth but most probably shows a jump upwards starting at the 24 hour data point” — sleep sits in the middle of it.

Roediger & Karpicke · Psych. Science · 2006

Same material, same number of exposures. One group re-read it. One group sat a recall test with no feedback.

Re-reading ahead
ON DELAYED TESTS

The authors also report that repeated studying raised students’ confidence in their ability to remember. The group that felt readiest retained less.

No per-interval retention percentage appears on this board. The “you forget 70% within 24 hours” figure that circulates with this topic is in neither paper, and we could not source it anywhere else.

Our read

“We covered that in training” is a statement about the calendar, not about what a rep can do at a door on Tuesday. Nothing in the block above is unusual and none of it is ours — it is ordinary practice in field sales. What the research settles is the order of the verbs: recall out loud beats review, and it beats it by more the longer you wait.
