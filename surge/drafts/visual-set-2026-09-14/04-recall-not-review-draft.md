## The post

> Everything taught in training week is sitting on a curve, and the curve was drawn in 1880.
>
> Murre and Dros published a full replication of Ebbinghaus in PLOS ONE in 2015: one subject, 70
> hours of learning, relearning tested at 20 minutes, 1 hour, 9 hours, 1 day, 2 days and 31 days.
> The curve held. They add one wrinkle — it is not perfectly smooth, and probably jumps back up at
> the 24-hour mark, with sleep sitting in the middle of it.
>
> The more useful study is Roediger and Karpicke, 2006. Same passage, same number of exposures. One
> group re-read it; one group sat a recall test with no feedback. Five minutes later, re-reading was
> ahead. On the delayed tests, recall was ahead substantially.
>
> The part worth sitting with: repeated studying raised students' confidence in their ability to
> remember. The group that felt readiest retained less.
>
> Our read: nobody forgets the pitch because they stopped caring. They forget it because that is
> what memory does. Which makes "we covered that in training" a statement about the calendar.
>
> What does your team actually do in the week after training week?

## Alternate hooks

1. *"Everything taught in training week is sitting on a curve, and the curve was drawn in 1880."*
   — used. The date makes forgetting structural rather than a failure of the rep, which is the whole
   point and also the kind thing to say.
2. *"Re-reading the manual is the study method that feels most like learning while doing the least
   of it."* — the sharpest line available. Held for the body: it lands harder once the experiment
   has been described.
3. *"Your new reps have already forgotten most of week one. That is not a discipline problem."*
   — warmer and more directly useful to a manager, but it opens on an assertion about their team.
4. *"Feeling prepared and being prepared are different things, and there is an experiment about it."*
   — good, but it oversells one well-replicated but bounded finding.

## Lines cut, with reasons

- *"This is why our training actually sticks."* — an outcome claim about Surge with no measurement
  behind it. §2 bars it and there is no internal figure that could rescue it.
- *"You forget 70% of it within 24 hours."* — **the exact content-farm number this standard exists
  to catch.** Traced only to retellings of Ebbinghaus, present in neither paper. Cut, and the cut is
  printed on the board rather than kept quiet.
- *"Most sales onboarding is a fire hose followed by a shrug."* — unsourced swipe at the category.
- *"Spaced repetition fixes this."* — true-ish and out of scope. Neither cited paper tests spacing;
  that is a separate literature and would need its own sourcing.
- *Any clock time on the morning block.* Not a line so much as a standing rule — see below.

**The deliberate omission, stated on the board.** No per-interval retention percentage appears
anywhere. The savings scores in the 2015 paper's tables were not read first-hand, and the figures
that circulate with this topic are mangled retellings. The interval strip therefore plots **when the
subject was re-tested**, which is published and verbatim, and says nothing about how much was left.

**Sleep attribution.** "Sleep sits in the middle of it" is our gloss on the 24-hour jump, not a
mechanism the abstract asserts. Phrased on the board as an observation about where the jump falls.

**Training-material check.** The four-step flow is the morning development block — goal-setting,
whiteboard drilling, one taught subject, paired practice on it, trainers coaching new reps. That is
on the **Clear to use** list in `RESTRICTED.md`, with one standing condition: **no clock times.**
None appear — no start time, no shift boundary, no duration, no weekly hours. The per-instance
sign-off gate on `training-source/` was lifted 2026-08-15, so no approval block is carried.
