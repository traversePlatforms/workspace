## The post

> A stranger at a door is worth 4.3 percentage points.
>
> That number comes from the randomised field experiments political scientists have been running on
> turnout for twenty-five years. Writing in Stanford Social Innovation Review, Alan Gerber and
> Gregory Huber line the channels up: a successful door-to-door contact lifts turnout by 4.3 points.
> A volunteer phone call, nearly 3. A commercial phone bank, under 1. A mailer, under half a point.
>
> Then they do the arithmetic that actually decides a campaign. About a quarter of the doors get
> answered. So across the whole list you set out to reach, the effect lands at a point or less.
>
> Our read: every number in that first paragraph is fixed before the day starts. The only one that
> moves is the last one, and it moves on how many doors got knocked. That is the law of averages
> with a coefficient attached to it, which is rarer than the phrase suggests.
>
> These measure voter turnout, not sales conversion. The shape transfers. The coefficient does not.
>
> What is the contact rate on your team's best channel, and does anybody actually measure it?

## Alternate hooks

1. *"A stranger at a door is worth 4.3 percentage points."* — used. Number first, and it is a number
   almost nobody in sales has seen, because it comes from a different literature.
2. *"The best-measured cold outreach in the world is not sales. It is voter turnout."* — frames the
   whole post in a line, but it spends the reader's attention on the premise instead of the finding.
3. *"Four channels, one honest comparison — and door-to-door wins by a distance nobody quotes."*
   — reads as a listicle opener.
4. *"Your pitch is not the variable. Your contact rate is."* — the thesis stated flat. Strong, but it
   asserts before it has earned anything, and it tells the reader what to think.

## Lines cut, with reasons

- *"Which is why field sales still works when everyone says it is dead."* — a verdict, and it argues
  with an unnamed critic. The chart makes the point without the defensiveness.
- *"We knock more doors than anyone in Colorado."* — a scale claim (Tier 2) and unverifiable. Gone.
- *"This is the maths behind our training."* — implies the research informs a Surge method. It does
  not; we found it, we did not run it, and §1 bars dressing borrowed evidence as proprietary insight.
- *"One vote for every 14 contacts."* — **the most quotable figure available, and deliberately not
  used.** See the fact-check log.
- *"Mail and cold calling are a waste of money."* — the data says weaker per contact, not worthless,
  and it says it about turnout. Overreach.

## Fact-check log

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Successful door-to-door contact increases turnout by 4.3 percentage points | **Verified** | Gerber & Huber, SSIR, 7 Mar 2016 — read first-hand 14 Sep 2026 |
| Typical contact rate ~25%, so the effect on the target group is "1 percentage point or less" | **Verified, near-verbatim** | Same article |
| Volunteer calls raise turnout by "nearly 3 percentage points" | **Verified, verbatim** | Same article |
| Commercial phone bank GOTV call: "less than 1 percentage point" | **Verified, verbatim — stated as a ceiling** | Same article |
| Typical non-partisan mailer: "less than 0.5 percentage points" | **Verified, verbatim — stated as a ceiling** | Same article |
| Alan Gerber is a co-author of the underlying Yale field-experiment programme | **Verified** | Author credit on the article; Gerber is the Gerber of Green & Gerber |

**Checked and rejected — the 1-in-14 rule of thumb.** Green and Gerber's *Get Out the Vote* is
widely quoted as finding one additional vote per 14 successful door-to-door contacts (with 35 for
phone banks, 66 for leaflets, 133 for mail), and a $19 cost per vote. Every route to it failed the
standard: the Chronicle of Philanthropy write-up 403s automated fetches, one PDF of the canvass
chapter is a scanned image with no extractable text, and a second is paywalled. It also **conflicts
with the SSIR figures** — 4.3 points per contact is roughly one vote per 23, not 14 — and the two
have different vintages and different definitions. `CLAUDE.md` says that where sources conflict and
none is authoritative, the detail comes out. It came out.

**On the 2016 date.** Ten years old and printed on the board for that reason. These are findings
from a mature experimental literature rather than a market statistic, so age is a caveat, not a
disqualification — but it is the reader's caveat to make, which means it has to be visible.

**Stated on the asset rather than hidden:** that two of the four figures are ceilings (drawn with a
hatched open end), and that the whole comparison measures turnout rather than sales.
