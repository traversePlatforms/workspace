Two studies, one of them 140 years old, on why the manual gets forgotten and what actually stops it.

1. The curve is real, and it was checked

Ebbinghaus mapped forgetting in the 1880s. Murre & Dros re-ran the whole thing and published the replication in PLOS ONE, 2015 — one subject, 70 hours of learning, relearning at six intervals.

2. What reverses it

Roediger & Karpicke, Psychological Science, 2006. Students read a passage, then either re-read it or sat a recall test with no feedback, the same number of times.

5 min laterRe-reading wins. 2 days laterTesting wins. 1 week laterTesting wins, substantially.

3. The trap in it

The same study measured how well prepared students felt.

Repeated studying increased their confidence in their ability to remember the material.

So the group that felt readiest was the group that retained less. Re-reading is the method that feels like learning while doing least of it.

Our read

Nobody forgets the pitch because they did not care. They forget it because that is what memory does, on a curve somebody drew before telephones were in houses.

Which makes "we covered that in training" a statement about the calendar, not about what a rep can do at a door. The morning block is drilling and paired practice on one taught subject for exactly this reason — recall out loud, not review.
