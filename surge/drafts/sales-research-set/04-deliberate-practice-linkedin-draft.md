## The post

> A number that a company which trains salespeople should be able to look at without flinching.
>
> Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, Psychological Science, 2014. A meta-analysis of how much deliberate practice explains differences in performance. Games: 26%. Music: 21%. Sports: 18%. Education: 4%. Professions: under 1%.
>
> The authors' own conclusion is that deliberate practice is important, but not as important as has been argued.
>
> Under 1% is the one that matters here, because sales is a profession.
>
> Our read: that is not evidence training does nothing. It is a measurement taken across domains where nobody has agreed what practice even is. A pianist running scales has a defined thing they are repeating and a clear way to tell whether it improved. In sales the practice target is usually "get better at the doors", and that is not a target, it is a hope.
>
> Which is the honest version of what a training operation is for. Not more practice. A named thing to practise, and a way to tell whether it moved.

## Read this before publishing

The post quotes a finding that, skim-read, says **training barely matters** — published by a
company that sells training. That is the whole reason it is interesting and the whole reason
it is risky.

Three things make it defensible as written:

1. **It does not hide the number.** Leading with "under 1%" and then explaining it is a much stronger position than omitting it and being shown it later.
2. **The explanation is the authors' territory, not a spin.** The professions estimate is low partly because "deliberate practice" is poorly operationalised in professional domains — that is a mainstream reading of the result, not a rescue.
3. **It ends on a claim about specificity, not superiority.** No suggestion Surge has solved this.

**The failure mode to watch:** a reader who takes away "even they admit training does not work."
If that is judged too likely, the fix is not to soften the number — it is to move the "our read"
earlier so the interpretation arrives with the statistic rather than after it. An alternate
running order is in the asset note.

**One thing I deliberately did not write:** any claim that Surge's training beats the 1%. There
is no measurement behind it, §2 bars it, and it would convert an honest post into the exact
thing §1 says never to claim.

## Alternate hooks

1. *"A number that a company which trains salespeople should be able to look at without flinching."* — used. Signals the awkwardness first, which buys the rest of the post.
2. *"Deliberate practice explains under 1% of performance differences in professions."* — the number naked. Strongest hook, highest risk of being screenshotted without the second half.
3. *"The 10,000-hours idea did not survive its own meta-analysis."* — most familiar entry point for a general reader, but it argues with Gladwell rather than saying anything about sales.
4. *"Most sales practice is not practice. It is repetition with no target."* — leads with the conclusion. Good, and it throws away the evidence that makes the conclusion credible.

## Lines cut, with reasons

- *"Which is why our training is different."* — a uniqueness claim, barred by §1, and unsupported.
- *"Talent matters more than anyone in this industry wants to admit."* — the meta-analysis does not say that. It says deliberate practice explains less variance than argued; what fills the rest is a separate question the paper does not settle.
- *"10,000 hours was always a myth."* — overstated and not what the paper tested. Ericsson's position and Gladwell's popularisation are different targets, and the study engages the first.
- *"If your reps are not improving, you are not training them, you are just scheduling them."* — the sharpest line written for this piece and it is a verdict aimed at the reader's team. Kept here because it may be worth using in a comment reply.

**Open item worth knowing about:** this meta-analysis is contested. Ericsson published a
response, and Macnamara and Hambrick replied to it in turn — there is a live methodological
argument about how deliberate practice should be defined and measured. **The post does not
mention the dispute**, which is defensible for a 200-word format quoting the headline result
and the authors' own conclusion, but it means a well-read commenter can raise it. The honest
reply, if it comes up, is that the dispute is about definition and measurement, which is
precisely the point the post is making.

The full-text PDF would not extract cleanly on two attempts, so all figures come from the
published abstract rather than the results tables. The five percentages and the concluding
sentence are all present there.
