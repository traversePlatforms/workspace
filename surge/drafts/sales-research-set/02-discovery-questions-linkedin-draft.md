## The post

> Every sales team has heard the number: 11 to 14 questions on a discovery call.
>
> It comes from Gong's analysis of more than 519,000 recorded B2B calls. Success peaks in that band and falls back to average above it.
>
> It is also the least useful part of the finding.
>
> The same research looked at where the questions sat, not just how many there were. Average reps front-load them like a checklist. Top performers spread them evenly across the call.
>
> Fourteen questions in the first four minutes is an intake form. The same fourteen spread out is a conversation. The count is identical and only one of them is discovery.
>
> Our read: a question count is easy to hand a new rep, and easy to hit while still running an interrogation. Where the questions sit is the part that takes coaching, and it is the part nobody quotes.
>
> One caveat we are not going to hide. This is B2B call data. Our reps work doorsteps and events, where the conversation is shorter and nobody booked it. The spread finding travels. The count probably does not.
>
> Where does your team's discovery actually sit?

## Alternate hooks

1. *"Every sales team has heard the number: 11 to 14 questions on a discovery call."* — used. Opens on the thing the reader already believes, which is what the post then takes apart.
2. *"The famous discovery-question number is the least useful part of the study it comes from."* — states the argument up front. Slightly more combative and gives away the turn too early.
3. *"Fourteen questions in four minutes is an intake form."* — the best image in the post, but as an opener it arrives without the research behind it.
4. *"519,000 calls, and everybody quoted the wrong half."* — punchy, and "wrong" is too strong for what is really a difference in usefulness.

## Lines cut, with reasons

- *"Most discovery training is a script with question marks in it."* — a swipe at the category, and unsourced.
- *"We teach reps to hold questions back."* — a training claim I cannot source, same problem as piece 1.
- *"Ask 12 and you will close more."* — states a causal promise the study does not support. The research reports association with success, not a lever.
- *"Nobody has ever bought anything because of question number thirteen."* — funny, and it argues against the count more than the evidence does.

## Fact-check log

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| More than 519,000 recorded B2B sales calls analysed | **Verified** | Gong, *Data Driven Tips to Mastering Sales Discovery Calls* — "over 519,000 B2B sales call recordings" |
| Success peaks at 11–14 targeted questions, drops to average above that | **Verified** | Same page |
| Average reps front-load questions like a checklist; top performers spread them evenly | **Verified** | Same page |
| Originally published 18 June 2017, last modified 4 March 2026 | **Verified** | Same page |
| "Fourteen questions in the first four minutes" | **Illustration, not a finding** | Written as an example. The study does not report a four-minute window |
| The finding transfers to doorstep and event selling | **Explicitly not claimed** | The post says the opposite, in the caveat. Deliberate — see below |

**The transfer question is the honest weak point of this piece and it is handled in the copy
rather than hidden.** Gong's corpus is recorded B2B calls, typically scheduled, typically long
enough to have a structure. Surge's field channels are none of those things. The spread finding
is about conversational shape and plausibly generalises; the 11–14 count is tied to call length
and almost certainly does not. Saying so is the post's credibility, not a weakness in it.

**Publication date, checked:** the piece is from 2017 with a 2026 modification stamp. The post
does not date the finding in the copy because the modification date makes "a 2017 study" mildly
misleading and "a 2026 study" plainly wrong. If a date is wanted, "Gong's discovery-call
analysis, first published in 2017" is the accurate phrasing.
