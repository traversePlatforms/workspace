## Post 1 — Personal ("I") · 172 words, ~185 once the slot is filled

The most common mistake I see in week one isn't nerves. It's talking too much.

New reps memorize the pitch and then treat the conversation as a delivery problem — get through it cleanly, handle whatever comes back. So they knock, the door opens, and they start presenting to someone who hasn't told them a single thing yet.

The fix is small and it feels wrong the first time. Ask one question before you pitch anything, then stop.

> **[YOUR QUESTION — one line]** The actual opening question you teach. Whatever it is, it should be something they can answer in a sentence without committing to anything.

Whatever comes back, the conversation now has somewhere to go. You're responding to a person instead of reciting at one.

What makes this hard isn't the technique. It's that the silence after a question feels like losing control, and the instinct is to fill it. Learning to leave it alone is most of the job.

We cover this on day one and people still fight it for weeks. The ones who get it stop sounding like they're selling and start sounding like they're useful.

What's the habit you had to unlearn when you started?

## Post 2 — Personal ("I") · 177 words, ~190 once the slot is filled

"I need to think about it" is the objection reps handle worst, because it doesn't sound like an objection. It sounds reasonable. So they accept it and leave.

In my experience it almost always means one of three things: they don't have the money, they can't make the decision alone, or something you said didn't land and they'd rather not argue about it. Those need completely different responses, and you can't choose one until you know which you're looking at.

So don't overcome it. Ask what they want to think about.

> **[YOUR PHRASING — one line]** How you actually teach this. The wording matters more here than anywhere else in the post, and mine would be a guess.

Asked without pressure, that usually gets you the real answer, and the real answer is something you can work with.

The reps who struggle here are trying to save the sale. The ones who do it well are trying to find out whether there is one, and they're comfortable if it turns out there isn't.

That comfort takes far longer to build than the script does, and it's the part that separates a good third month from a bad one.

What objection do you hear most?

## Post 3 — Company ("we") · 180 words · plainer register

Our training manual has a section on handling rejection. It's about two pages and it's fine. It says roughly what you'd expect: don't take it personally, it isn't about you, go to the next door.

Two pages of that does almost nothing on an actual doorstep.

What works is volume plus a specific debrief. A new rep goes out with someone experienced, hears no a lot, and then afterward walks back through three of them. What was said, what they did, what they'd change next time. Not to fix their attitude. To turn a wall of rejection into a short list of things they can adjust.

The manual is still worth having. It gives everyone the same language and the same starting point. But it teaches the concept, not the tolerance, and only one of those gets somebody through their second month.

Closing that gap is most of what we actually do — the same way for a nonprofit campaign as for a roofing or HVAC team.

How does your team teach the part that doesn't fit in a manual?
