## The post

> At Surge you don't get to train someone on their first day until you've trained a lot of other days first.
>
> The order runs backwards. You start with someone in their second week, then their first week, then day three, then day two. Day one comes last.
>
> When I first saw that I assumed it was a seniority thing. It isn't. Day one is just the hardest teaching in the building — the person has no context, no reason to trust you yet, and they mostly decide what they think of the job in the first few hours. Week two is forgiving because they already know the shape of the work. So the ladder is ordered by how hard the teaching is, not by how long you've been around.
>
> There's a line that goes with it. Do the job, explain the job, teach the job. I don't think that's original to us and I'm not going to pretend it is. But I hadn't seen anyone build the sequence backwards like that before, and it changed how I think about who should be teaching what.
>
> What's the thing at your company that only your best people are allowed to do?

## Alternate hooks

1. *"At Surge you don't get to train someone on their first day until you've trained a lot of other days first."* — used. Concrete, slightly odd, makes you want the explanation.
2. *"The hardest hour to teach at our company is the first one, so it's the last one you're allowed to teach."* — tighter, more symmetrical. Cut because the symmetry is a rhetorical move and §6 says he doesn't do those.
3. *"We train our trainers backwards."* — punchy but it's a landing line, which §6 explicitly rules out.
4. *"Most companies let their newest trainer take the newest hire. That's backwards, and we do it the other way round."* — sets up a contrast with unnamed others. Weak because it's an implicit verdict on companies we can't name.

## Lines cut, with reasons

- *"Nobody designs that by accident."* — good line, but it's a flourish and it lands the paragraph. §6: no landing lines.
- *"That's the whole difference between knowing something and being able to hand it to someone else."* — too neat, resolves the thought. He concedes and stops; he doesn't resolve.
- *"You earn the right to teach a first day."* — "earn the right" edges into advancement framing, which is Tier 2 restricted.
- *"It takes about two months to get there."* — any timeline invites the quota and hours questions. Cut on RESTRICTED grounds.
- *"Our trainers are certified."* — true (there's a certificate and a speech) but reads as a credential claim and invites "certified by whom?"
- *"Most sales floors don't think this hard about onboarding."* — unnamed-competitor verdict. Company spec §3.

## Voice notes — what this draft is testing

Deliberate applications of the rewritten §6:

- **Concession without resolution** — "I don't think that's original to us and I'm not going to pretend it is. But I hadn't seen…" This is the dominant pattern and it also discharges the §1 never-claim-proprietary rule in his own voice rather than by omission.
- **Hedging** — "I assumed," "I don't think," "mostly decide."
- **Trailing opinion** — "…and it changed how I think about who should be teaching what," tacked on rather than given its own sentence.
- **Concrete before abstract** — the sequence is stated before any principle is drawn from it.
- **No landing line.** The paragraph before the question ends on a subordinate clause on purpose. It will feel underpowered against a normal LinkedIn post. That's the test.
- **Genuinely open closing question** — not engagement bait; it's a question he might actually want an answer to.
- **No em-dash contrast, no "isn't about X — it's about Y," no named value, no warmth added.**

**What to react to:** the flatness of the ending is the thing to judge. If it reads as weak rather than as plain, §6 has overcorrected and I'll pull it back toward the middle.
