## The post

**Eyebrow:** Blog

# Good feedback is something you can use tomorrow.

**Lede:** Most feedback gets a nod and changes nothing. The notes that stick are small and specific, and given
while the moment is still fresh.

### Why the nod isn't enough

Almost everyone has given or received a note like "be more confident" or "good energy today." Both are well
meant. Neither tells the person what to do next time.

"Be more confident" describes how someone came across. It doesn't say what they did to come across that way, so
there's nothing to change. The person agrees, and the next conversation looks exactly like the last one. Praise
has the same problem in reverse. "Great job" feels good for a minute, but nobody can repeat something that was
never named.

A good test for any note is whether the person could act on it in their very next conversation. If they
couldn't, it was a reaction. A useful note is an instruction for next time.

numbers, no client or sector detail.]

### Point at the moment, not the person

The quickest way to make a note specific is to tie it to a moment. "You need to listen more" is about the
person, and people tend to defend themselves against that. "When she asked how long it would take, you started
answering before she'd finished the question" is about one moment, and anyone can picture doing it differently.

You can see behavior, so you can describe it, and anything you can describe, someone can practice. A trait only
gets argued about.

The same goes for praise. "When he pushed back, you paused and let him finish before you answered. Keep doing
that." Now the person knows exactly what worked, and they'll do it again on purpose.

### One note at a time

After watching someone work, it's tempting to hand over everything you noticed. Six notes feel thorough. The
person then walks into their next conversation trying to hold six things in their head, and most of them fall
away.

Pick the one that matters most. Usually it's the one that takes a few of the others with it once it changes. A
person who stops rushing the opening of a conversation often stops rushing the rest of it too. The other five
can wait for next week, when the first change has had time to stick.

### Say it while it's fresh

A note given a week later is a history lesson. Neither of you remembers the moment clearly, and the habit has
had a week to settle in.

The same note given right after the conversation lands differently. The moment is still clear to both of you,
and the next chance to try it is close. Short and soon does more than long and late.

### Have them try it, right then

A note isn't finished when it's been said. It's finished when the person has done the new version once. "Let's
run that back. I'll ask the question, and you let me finish before you answer." That one line turns advice into
practice.

Hearing a note and doing it once are a long way apart. The first try is usually a little clumsy. The second is
easier, and by the third it starts to sound like them.

### Ask for it the same way

All of this works in the other direction too. A leader who asks "any feedback?" at the end of a meeting will
almost always hear "no, all good." It's an easy question to answer without thinking. A specific question gets a
real answer: "What's one thing from this morning I could have explained better?"

How a leader takes the answer matters as much as asking. Thank them, skip the explanation, and try the change
in the next meeting. People watch how feedback is received before they decide how much of it to give.

[ANECDOTE: a time someone newer on the team gave a leader a specific note about a meeting or a training session,
and what the leader changed afterward. Two or three sentences. No names. Optional: the section reads without it.]

### The note is about the next one

Feedback isn't a verdict on a person. It's one moment, named clearly, so the next one goes a little better.
Keep each note to one thing. Give it close to the moment, and let the person try it before you move on. Teams
that work this way get better a little every day, one specific note at a time.
